# California Workers’ Comp Submission Workflow – Forms, Data & Carrier Insights

**For an independent agency writing workers’ compensation in California (especially construction/contracting risks), a thorough submission workflow is critical.** Below we break down the process step-by-step – from completing ACORD applications and gathering detailed data, to navigating carrier portals, supplemental forms, loss runs, and even renewals. Short, focused sections and lists highlight the key **forms, data groupings, carrier landscape, and the role of loss history** in a successful submission.

## Essential ACORD Forms for Workers’ Comp in CA

**ACORD standardized applications are the backbone of a workers’ comp submission.** California submissions typically use **ACORD 125** (Commercial Insurance Application – common info) and the **ACORD 130** Workers’ Compensation section (including a CA-specific version with state-required notices). ACORD 125 acts as a cover sheet with general business details, while ACORD 130 provides the line-specific workers’ comp information. Together, these forms give underwriters a consistent, complete snapshot of the risk across carriers.

- **ACORD 125 (Applicant Information Common Section):** Captures the insured’s basic information – business name, addresses, FEIN, contact info, legal entity type, etc. It also includes prior insurance and loss history in brief. Think of this as the **“front page”** of the submission, used for _all_ commercial lines (GL, Property, Auto, WC, etc.).
    
- **ACORD 130 (Workers’ Compensation Application):** The dedicated workers’ comp form consolidating all **exposure, payroll, and operations data** needed for underwriting. In California, the ACORD 130 CA version includes the state’s required fraud warning and acknowledgments. This form details the WC-specific info not on ACORD 125, such as class codes, payroll by class, experience mod, and safety practices (see next section for field breakdown).
    

**Additional forms**: Many carriers accept just the ACORD 125 and 130 for a complete WC submission. If other lines are quoted concurrently, their ACORD sections (e.g. 126 for GL) accompany the 125 – but for monoline workers’ comp, ACORD 130 and 125 suffice. California may have unique requirements (e.g. a state form to exclude corporate officers if applicable), but those are usually handled at binding. **Be sure to use the latest ACORD editions and the correct state-specific version** to include California’s mandated language. (Using an outdated or wrong-state form can cause compliance issues.)

## Key Data Groupings & Required Fields

A California WC application demands **granular data** about the business and its exposures. An insurance producer will gather and input information grouped into several sections on the ACORD forms:

- **Applicant & Business Details:** The legal **name of the insured, FEIN**, and entity type (corp, LLC, partnership, etc.). Include the mailing and physical addresses for all locations (with ZIP codes) and years in business. Providing the **NAICS/SIC industry code** is crucial for proper risk classification. _(Tip: ensure the FEIN and NAICS code are accurate and consistent – carriers use these to verify operations and experience mods.)_
    
- **Description of Operations:** A narrative of what the company does, highlighting its **primary work activities and any high-risk tasks**. For a **contractor**, this means specifying the type of construction (e.g. residential remodeling, commercial electrical work, roofing, etc.), typical job sites, and any specialty services. A clear, detailed description (e.g. _“Plumbing contractor – installs and repairs piping in residential/commercial buildings, no work over 2 stories”_) helps underwriters understand the exposure. This section may be entered on ACORD 125 and expanded in Remarks if needed.
    
- **Locations & Classifications:** List **all work locations** or states of operation, and for each, list the applicable **WC classification codes** and their descriptions. Each class code represents a type of labor performed (e.g. code for carpentry, roofing, clerical, etc.). If the business has multiple job sites, each address is scheduled with appropriate class codes. In construction, include any separate shop/yard location versus field job sites. Indicate if any work is performed out-of-state or at insured’s client sites (this ensures coverage is filed for all states where employees work).
    
- **Payroll and Employee Data:** For each class code, provide the **estimated annual payroll** and number of employees for the coming policy term. Payroll is typically broken down by class code because premiums are calculated per $100 of payroll in each classification. You’ll separate **clerical or office payroll vs. field payroll**, etc. (Underestimating payroll can lead to surprises at audit – use realistic figures, such as last year’s audited payroll, to avoid large adjustments.) Also note the total number of employees (full-time, part-time, seasonal) in each class.
    
- **Owners, Officers & Exclusions:** List all **owners, partners, and executive officers** of the company along with their titles, ownership percentage, and whether they are to be **included or excluded** from workers’ comp coverage. In California, company owners can elect to exclude themselves if the entity structure and ownership share meet statutory requirements. The ACORD 130 has a section to denote each officer and a checkbox if excluded. This is important because excluding an owner removes their payroll from rating (lowering premium), but requires a separate waiver form at binding. Underwriters verify these details to ensure proper rating and eligibility (e.g. some very small firms _must_ include owners unless they qualify for exclusion).
    
- **Coverage and Policy Details:** Specify the **requested policy term** (effective and expiration dates) and any special coverage options. Workers’ comp in CA is typically “guaranteed cost” (no deductible), but if the insured seeks a deductible or higher employer’s liability limits, that can be noted. ACORD 130 also lets you indicate if this submission is assigned risk or voluntary market, and the employer’s liability limit options (e.g. $1M/$1M/$1M limits are common). These fields ensure the quote is set up with the right coverage parameters. Billing and audit info may be recorded too (who to bill, installment plan, and the audit contact).
    
- **Prior Insurance History:** A 5-year history of **previous workers’ comp carriers**, policy numbers, and premiums paid. This is listed on ACORD 125 and/or 130. Underwriters use it to gauge if the account has stable coverage or jumped around, and to see premium trends. Include the full policy term dates for each year. Any **lapses** or cancellations should be explained, as they are red flags. Consistency is key – this section should align with the loss run records provided.
    
- **Experience Modification (X-Mod):** The current **experience mod factor** (if applicable) for the insured, usually a number like 0.85, 1.00, 1.25 etc., is a critical field. ACORD 130 has a space for the mod for the upcoming year and sometimes for prior years. In CA, the WCIRB issues an X-Mod annually for qualifying businesses (generally if they’ve been insured for over 2 years and meet premium thresholds). This factor adjusts premium up or down based on past loss performance. A **verified copy of the Experience Rating Worksheet** from the WCIRB or NCCI (in other states) is often requested to substantiate the mod. Carriers will apply this factor to quoted rates, so providing it up front is important for an accurate quote.
    
- **Loss History:** A summary of **claims over the past 3–5 years**. ACORD forms provide table columns to list the number of claims and total incurred losses (paid + reserved) each year. You should indicate the valuation date of this data (e.g. “losses valued as of Dec 31, 2025”) and check the box if **“Loss Run Attached”**. Significant or large individual claims should be explained in the Remarks section or an attachment. _Example:_ if there was a $250,000 loss due to a fall, note what happened and any corrective action (underwriters appreciate context on large losses). In practice, detailed **loss run reports** from prior carriers are attached to provide full loss details beyond these summary numbers.
    
- **General Underwriting Questions:** A series of Yes/No questions that uncover additional exposures or background. These appear on ACORD 130 (often on the later pages) and **must be answered for a complete submission**. Common questions include:
    
    - Does the applicant own, operate or lease aircraft or watercraft? (Potential exposure outside normal WC scope)
        
    - Any work at heights, above a certain feet, or on vessels, docks, over water, underground, etc? (Hazardous conditions for employees)
        
    - Does the company handle **hazardous materials** or waste?
        
    - Are sub-contractors used in the business? If yes, what percent of work is subcontracted out, and are certificates of insurance obtained from those subs? (Especially crucial for contractors – underwriters want to ensure subcontractors have their own WC insurance so the exposure isn’t covered under the insured’s policy)
        
    - Any work performed under/about water, or underground (tunneling), or involving heights over X feet?
        
    - Are there any **seasonal employees** or volunteer workers?
        
    - Does the insured have a **formal safety program** or written injury & illness prevention program?
        
    - Is there a **return-to-work program** for injured employees? (Facilitating light duty can reduce claims costs)
        
    - Does the insured conduct **drug testing** for employees?
        
    - Any past **policy cancellations or non-renewals** (other than carrier withdrawing from market)?
        
    - Any undisputed unpaid WC premium owed by the insured to past carriers? (This checks for history of premium non-payment)
        
    - Do they provide group transportation for employees (e.g. a crew bus to job sites)?
        
    - Do employees travel out of state or internationally for work?
        
    - Is the business involved in any other operations or owned by another entity? etc.
        
    
    For any “Yes” answer, the agent **must provide explanation** in the Remarks (or on an ACORD 101 additional page). For example, if “sub-contractors used” = Yes, the remarks should detail the % of work subcontracted and that all subs carry their own comp coverage (and maybe attach a subcontractor agreement sample). Thorough explanations prevent delays – an underwriter will **follow up on any yes** answers not fully clarified.
    
- **Premium and Rating Information:** The ACORD 130 form includes a section where the **estimated premium** is calculated. Typically, this section will be filled in by the underwriter, but it’s worth noting what it includes: the sum of each class code’s manual premium (rate × payroll), then adjusted by the experience mod, any schedule rating credits/debits, deductibles, state fees, etc., to arrive at estimated annual premium. The producer usually doesn’t fill out the dollar amounts, but should ensure any applicable credits or premium adjustments (like a Contractor’s credit or premium discount for larger accounts) are discussed with underwriters. In California, additional assessments (for state funds, fraud assessment, etc.) will be added by carriers as required – they aren’t inputs on the form but part of the final premium.
    
- **Signatures and Certification:** Finally, the application must be **signed and dated** by both the applicant (an officer/owner of the insured company) and the producing agent/broker. The ACORD forms for CA include the **fraud warning statement** and any required state-specific text that the signer is attesting to. By signing, the client confirms the info is truthful and accurate. Missing signatures can stall a submission, so producers must obtain signed apps (wet signature or electronic acceptable) before binding coverage. The producer’s signature and license number also usually go on the form, affirming they are licensed in CA and the information was gathered in accordance with regulations.
    

**Data dependencies:** It’s important that all these pieces of information align logically. For instance, the operations description should correlate with the class codes used (e.g. if described as a roofing contractor, one of the class codes should indeed be the roofing code – otherwise something is wrong). The **prior carrier and loss history** should match the provided loss runs; any large losses on the loss runs should appear in the loss history summary. If subcontracting is indicated, the underwriter will expect either a lower payroll (because subs do some work) or clarity that subs are independent – inconsistencies can trigger additional questions. In short, a well-done submission ties the narrative, exposure data, and historical data together into a coherent story of the risk.

## Supplemental Forms and Questionnaires (Construction Focus)

**Many carriers require supplemental questionnaires** in addition to the ACORD forms, especially for high-risk industries like construction. These **“supplementals”** gather more granular detail about the account’s operations, safety, and loss controls – often going beyond what ACORD forms cover. An independent producer will often pre-fill such supplements or have the client complete them to strengthen the submission.

- **Contractor’s Supplemental Application:** Nearly all insurers writing construction will ask for a contractors supplemental (sometimes a carrier-specific form). This form digs into specifics such as:
    
    - **Detailed breakdown of work:** e.g. percentage of work that is new construction vs. remodel, residential vs. commercial, interior vs. exterior, etc. (must total 100%). This helps underwriters gauge the hazard level – e.g. exterior work at heights is riskier, and residential construction might have different risks than commercial.
        
    - **Maximum project heights or depths:** e.g. what is the tallest building or structure they work on (or deepest excavation) – to assess severity potential (falls, etc.).
        
    - **Use of subcontractors:** what % of total work is subcontracted out; does the insured obtain certificates of WC insurance from all subs; any uninsured subs or 1099 labor used? _(Using uninsured subcontractors can effectively make the insured responsible for those workers’ comp risks, so carriers scrutinize this.)_
        
    - **Payroll, receipts, and subcontractor cost history:** Some supplements include tables to enter the past 3-5 years of payroll, gross sales/receipts, and cost of subcontracted work. This mirrors data that might factor into premium audits and shows growth or trends in the business. **Example:** AmTrust’s contractors supplemental specifically asks for historical payroll and receipts by year, and the cost of any temps or subcontracted labor. This helps underwriters see if payroll is increasing (indicating business growth) or if a lot of work is outsourced to subs.
        
    - **Owners’/officers’ involvement in field work:** e.g. how much of the payroll is for owners actively on job sites vs. office-only. If an owner is excluded from coverage but still occasionally works in the field, that’s a risk factor to discuss.
        
    - **Licensing and experience:** confirmation that the contractor holds a valid license (provide license number) and how many years of experience in the trade. Carriers may decline inexperienced or unlicensed contractors. For example, ICW Group’s supplemental asks if the insured has had workers’ comp for at least 3 years and for the active contractor’s license number and years in business.
        
    - **Safety practices:** questions about formal safety programs, OSHA training, use of personal protective equipment, frequency of safety meetings, etc. Construction carriers want assurance that the firm prioritizes safety (fall protection, scaffolding safety, etc. for contractors).
        
    - **Equipment and special exposures:** e.g. does the insured use any cranes, lifts, or heavy equipment? Any work involving asbestos, lead, or other hazardous materials? Any roofing operations or tree trimming? These specifics might not be fully revealed on the ACORD, so the supplement asks them directly.
        
    - **Prior loss analysis:** if there were large claims, some supplements ask for a description of what happened and what’s been done to prevent recurrence. This is the insured’s chance to explain improvements made after a big loss (e.g. “Implemented a fall harness program after a fall injury in 2022”) which can help get favorable consideration despite past losses.
        
    
    Overall, the contractor supplemental helps underwriters judge if this construction risk fits their appetite. It essentially **fills in the gaps** – e.g., ACORD might just show payroll and class code for “roofing”, but the supplement will clarify if the roofer does residential only, max three-story buildings, uses safety harnesses, and subcontracts out any torch-down hot roofing, etc., which hugely affects risk.
    
- **Carrier-Specific WC Supplement:** Even outside construction, some carriers have a general workers’ comp supplemental application. These often cover similar ground as ACORD but may ask additional questions like: Does the employer have a human resources manual? Pre-hire physicals? A designated medical provider clinic? Any prior coverage with this carrier or cancellations? If the ACORD was fully filled, a general supplement might not be needed, but _some carriers require their version be used or certain sections be completed_. For example, one insurer’s supplemental might ask if the company is a **union shop** (union contractors sometimes have different considerations) or if they use any **employee leasing/PEO** arrangements.
    
- **Program/Wholesaler Applications:** If an independent agent is accessing a market via a wholesaler or MGA, that intermediary might have a **standardized submission checklist or app**. For instance, a wholesaler might provide a “Workers’ Comp submission cover sheet” requiring the ACORD forms plus their own questionnaire. These forms ensure nothing is missed (like asking if the risk has been declined by other carriers, or if they are open to higher deductibles, etc.).
    

**Important:** From a workflow perspective, a savvy producer will **gather all this info at once** from the client. Rather than doing the bare minimum on ACORD and then reacting to underwriter questions, it’s best to proactively complete relevant supplements for the class of business. This makes the submission _“complete”_ and more likely to get quick, competitive quotes. Underwriters often prioritize submissions that are thorough and require no additional info (whereas an incomplete submission can sit waiting for clarification).

## Carrier Landscape in California and Submission Portals

**California’s workers’ comp market is robust**, with a mix of national carriers, specialty insurers, and the state’s own fund. As an independent agency, you typically will approach multiple carriers to find the best terms, especially for construction accounts. Understanding the **carrier landscape** and their submission processes is key:

- **Major WC Carriers in CA:** Big national insurers like **Travelers, The Hartford, Liberty Mutual, Zurich, and CNA** are active in California and appoint independent agents to offer workers’ comp. There are also specialty carriers focusing on workers’ comp, such as **ICW Group (Insurance Company of the West)** and **Zenith** (both known for insuring California construction and agriculture risks), **Employers Insurance (EIG)** which targets small businesses, **Berkshire Hathaway GUARD**, **AmTrust**, and **Omaha National**, among others. Many of these were highlighted as top workers’ comp providers in 2025, reflecting strong broker feedback.
    
- **State Compensation Insurance Fund (State Fund):** California’s State Fund is a significant player – a state-run insurer of last resort that will write higher-risk businesses or new ventures that private carriers decline. Independent brokers can submit to State Fund (you need to be registered with them). State Fund has its own online portal (**State Fund Online**) where agents enter quote info. They also introduced a **BindNow** feature for instant binding on certain small policies. For many contractors that struggle to find coverage (e.g. roofing contractors with losses), State Fund might be the fallback. The submission to State Fund still uses the same data (they even accept ACORD apps), but brokers typically key it into their system.
    
- **Wholesalers and MGA Programs:** Some markets are accessed via wholesalers like **AmWINS, RT Specialty, RPS, or BTIS** (which is an MGA). These intermediaries might have exclusive programs for tough classes or better rates. For example, AmWINS might have a program with State Fund or a specific carrier for high-risk construction. When using a wholesaler, the agent sends the ACORD apps and supplements to the wholesaler, who then submits to the carrier on the agent’s behalf. Each wholesaler may have its own submission portal or email system.
    
- **Carrier Portals (“InsurTech” Integration):** Nearly all standard carriers have web portals where agents can **input submission data directly**. For small businesses, some carriers even offer instant quotes online. For instance, Travelers and Hartford have platforms where you fill in the ACORD information fields in a web form and get a quote indication if the risk fits their appetite. These portals mirror the data on the ACORD 125/130 – you’ll be asked the same questions: business info, class codes, payroll, prior coverage, loss history, etc. Many carriers **require** portal submission for certain small accounts and will not accept email applications for those. Others allow email submissions of ACORD PDFs to an underwriter, especially for larger or more complex accounts that need underwriter review.
    
- **Key Portal Features:** When using a portal, be prepared for some carrier-specific nuances. For example, one carrier’s system might automatically pull the experience mod from the rating bureau if you input the FEIN, while another requires you to type it in. Some portals might have extra underwriting questions not on the ACORD (e.g. “Has the insured been with their current carrier for at least 3 years?” or “Any past bankruptcy?”) – essentially the same info that could be in a supplemental. The goal of each portal is to capture all info needed to quote. As a producer, having **all your data gathered upfront** means you can quickly populate any portal. If you’ve filled out the ACORD and a contractor’s supplemental thoroughly, you can copy-paste answers into whatever system.
    
- **Consolidation Tools:** Because re-keying data into multiple carrier portals is time-consuming, agencies often look for ways to **consolidate the submission process**. One approach is using comparative rating platforms or upload tools. For instance, **Semsee** and similar InsurTech platforms let an agent input the data (or upload ACORD forms) once and then **distribute it to multiple carriers** for quotes. These platforms integrate with carrier APIs or email systems, reducing duplicate entry. They accept ACORD 125/130 PDF uploads and parse them to populate quotes across carriers that participate. This is extremely useful in a market like CA with many carriers – you might, for example, fill out the info in Semsee and simultaneously get quotes or submissions going with, say, Travelers, Guard, Employers, and ICW Group.
    
- **Carrier-Specific Supplementals:** Keep in mind that even with consolidation tools, some carriers will still come back requesting their own supplemental forms or additional info for certain classes. For example, **AmTrust** might not release a firm quote until their specific contractor questionnaire is provided (as we saw, it asks for detailed breakdowns and history). **ICW Group** might want their supplemental if the risk is mid-sized or larger construction, to evaluate safety and operational details. Thus, the agent should be ready to **upload attachments** (PDF supplements, loss runs, etc.) in the portal or email. Most carrier portals have an “attachments” section where you can upload these supporting docs.
    
- **Turnaround and Communication:** Using portals can speed up quoting (some give instant rates if criteria are met), but for more complex cases the submission goes to an underwriter for review. It’s good practice to follow up with underwriters at different carriers once you’ve submitted, to confirm they have everything needed. If a carrier’s appetite is questionable (e.g. a very high-risk roofing contractor to a standard carrier), the portal might decline upfront – so you’d know to pivot to surplus lines or State Fund. Knowing which carriers are **best for construction** helps target the submissions: e.g. **Travelers and ICW** are known for contracting appetite (with good safety programs), **Hartford** might target lower-hazard contractors (electrical, plumbing but not roofing), **Zurich**/**CNA** focus on larger construction firms, while **State Fund** and some surplus lines will handle the tough or high-mod accounts. The 2025 top list included many of these players for California, showing they are actively writing business.
    

**Bottom line:** The independent agent’s workflow is to **collect one comprehensive set of data and documentation, then repurpose it across multiple carriers’ submissions.** The ACORD forms serve as the universal template that all carriers recognize. Carrier portals and supplementals are just different fronts for inputting that same core data. By preparing detailed ACORD apps and key supplements (and having loss runs ready), an agent can efficiently navigate each carrier’s process – whether that’s an online form, an email to an underwriter, or an upload to a multi-carrier system. This consolidated approach not only saves time but also ensures consistency (avoiding errors from typing info multiple times).

## The Role of Loss Runs and Loss History in Submissions

**Loss runs are a critical component of a workers’ comp submission**, as they provide the insurer with the business’s actual claims experience. Here’s how they factor in:

- **Definition:** A **loss run** is an official report from a prior insurance carrier listing all claims on the policy during a period, including dates of loss, description, amounts paid, reserves outstanding, and status of each claim. Typically, agents gather **3 to 5 years of loss runs** for a new business submission – this aligns with the prior policy history provided on ACORD 125/130.
    
- **Carrier Requirement:** Virtually all underwriters will **require recent loss runs** to quote or bind coverage. In fact, providing them upfront with the submission is best. A complete submission includes **“hard copy” loss runs for 3-5 years, valued within the last 90 days**. If loss runs are not included, many carriers will issue only a **tentative quote** subject to receiving acceptable loss runs, or they may not quote at all until they see them.
    
- **In California**, the importance of loss runs is underscored by regulation: state law gives policyholders (or their brokers) the right to obtain loss run reports from the current insurer promptly. **Insurers must furnish loss runs within 10 business days of a written request** when a policy is canceled, non-renewed, or within 60 days of renewal, etc.. This helps insureds shop their coverage. As a producer, you should advise your client to request these early (or request on their behalf) so you have them in time for marketing the account.
    
- **Assessing Risk:** Underwriters analyze loss runs to spot frequency (many small injuries?) and severity (large costly claims?) trends. A **clean loss history** (few or no claims) is a big positive – it can lead to better pricing or credits. On ACORD 130, you’ll note the total claims count and total incurred each year, but the detailed loss runs let the underwriter verify those figures and see the nature of claims. For example, they can see if all claims were minor medical-only claims or if there were serious accidents. They also check if any claims remain open (reserved) which could indicate ongoing issues.
    
- **Experience Mod correlation:** The loss history ties directly into the **experience modification factor**. The mod is essentially a numeric representation of how the insured’s losses compare to average, and it’s calculated from past losses reported to the rating bureau. Carriers will cross-check the provided loss runs against the experience mod worksheet. If the insured has a high mod (above 1.00), it signals worse-than-average losses; underwriters will scrutinize the loss runs to understand why. Conversely, a low mod (below 1.00) backed by clean loss runs can make the risk more desirable.
    
- **Presentation of Loss Info:** If there have been significant losses, a good submission does more than just attach loss runs. The agent should **provide context or narratives for large claims**. For example, if there was a big roofing injury claim, explain what safety measures were implemented after that incident. Loss runs alone are just raw data – explaining the _story_ behind them can reassure underwriters that the problem causing the loss has been addressed. In addition, if the loss runs show a major claim that appears anomalous, highlight that (e.g. one-off event, not likely to recur). Some agents even include a cover letter with the submission summarizing losses and improvements made.
    
- **Use in Pricing:** Loss runs influence the quote beyond just the mod. If an account has frequent small claims, a carrier might still quote but maybe add a surcharge or be cautious with credits. If there are very large losses, some carriers may decline the risk outright (or only consider it under a higher-deductible plan or in the assigned risk pool). On the other hand, **excellent loss history** can lead to schedule credits or even dividend plan offerings (where the insured can get money back for good future loss performance). Underwriters also use loss runs to verify payroll in some cases (e.g. if an employer had very low reported payroll but had a high number of injuries, something might be off).
    
- **Loss Run Logistics:** As mentioned, getting current-valued loss runs is important. They should be **on the carrier’s letterhead** and show valuation dates. An independent agent will often assist the client in requesting these from their current or prior insurers. Many carriers have online broker portals to download loss runs instantly. Others require an email to a dedicated inbox. Given the 10-day legal requirement in CA, carriers will comply, but one shouldn’t wait until the last minute. It’s best to have loss runs ready a month or two before renewal to shop the account.
    
- **Loss Runs for Renewals:** If the agent is **renewing with the same carrier**, the carrier obviously has the loss information internally (and will factor it into the renewal pricing). The agent may not need to obtain official loss runs in that case, though it’s still good practice to have them for discussion. If **remarketing at renewal** (moving to a new carrier), then treat it like new business – you’ll again need up-to-date loss runs from the expiring carrier to give to competing markets.
    
- **Common requirement:** Most carriers ask for **currently valued loss runs for at least 3 years** (5 years is even better). “Currently valued” means dated usually within the last 3-6 months. For example, if you’re submitting in March 2026 and the policy renews June 1, 2026, loss runs should be valued no earlier than, say, January 2026. If only older ones are available, the underwriter may ask for an updated report closer to effective date. Also, if the insured had any **large losses**, carriers might specifically ask for loss details or **OSHA logs** if it’s a very large account, but that’s less common in standard submissions.
    

In summary, **loss runs are essential to tell the insured’s claims story**. They are attached to the ACORD submission as proof of the loss history you’ve summarized. A solid submission will highlight good performance or explain bad performance. Having them organized and ready demonstrates professionalism and speeds up the quoting process. _Remember:_ in California, you have tools (like the Insurance Code 11663.5) to get those loss runs quickly when needed – leverage that so you never submit without this piece of the puzzle.

## New Business vs. Renewal Workflow Considerations

The above workflow applies mainly to **new business submissions** (submitting an account to a new carrier). For completeness, here’s how **renewals** are handled, and how the process overlaps or differs:

- **Renewal with the Same Carrier:** When it’s time to renew a policy, the **incumbent carrier** will typically prepare a renewal quote based on updated payroll and the latest experience mod. Often, 90-120 days out, they’ll send the agent a **renewal exposure questionnaire** or rely on the recent audit to update payroll for the new term. The agent’s role is to **update any changes**: new operations, added locations, changes in employee count or class codes, etc. This may involve filling out a short renewal update form rather than a full ACORD application every year (some carriers might still want a new signed ACORD if there are major changes or just as a best practice, but many will renew based on expiring info adjusted for new exposure). It’s wise for the producer to confirm **any material changes** with the insured each year – e.g. “Have you started any new types of work? Any new states? Did you purchase any other companies?” – and inform the underwriter. If something significant changed (say a contractor that previously did no roofing now does some roofing), a new supplemental or application update would be needed to reflect that.
    
- **Marketing the Renewal (Remarketing):** If the current carrier is non-renewing, or if their renewal quote is uncompetitive, the agent may shop the account to **new carriers at renewal**. In that case, the process is essentially the **same as a new business submission** – you’ll dust off the ACORD 125 and 130 (or create new ones) and ensure all info is updated for the new policy period, then send to other carriers. One advantage on renewal is you often have **actual audited payrolls** and exact loss runs for the past term, which provides more certainty than initial estimates. Carriers quoting the renewal will require the same data set: updated payroll estimates, the current experience mod, and loss runs valued likely up to near the renewal date.
    
- **Lead Time:** Renewals should be started early. For construction risks in CA, it’s common to start marketing the renewal 60-90 days in advance. This allows time to get loss runs (if the insured is switching, recall that within 60 days of renewal you can request them and the carrier must comply in 10 days), complete any new supplemental questions, and negotiate with underwriters. Some carriers will hold quotes for a period or may improve terms if they know it’s a renewal in competition.
    
- **Differences in Data Gathering:** On a renewal with the same carrier, you usually **don’t need to resend all the historical data** that the carrier already has (like prior loss history) – they have it. The focus is on **changes since last year**: e.g. updated payroll, any new class codes or operations, any large losses that occurred during the policy term and how they were handled. Many carriers send a renewal **application update form** asking if any new entities or ownership changes occurred, any new locations, etc., and confirmation of key data. The producer can often simply update the ACORD 130 from last year with any changes, get the client to sign it, and submit that as a narrative of changes.
    
- **Pricing and Losses on Renewal:** The current carrier will use the new experience mod (which incorporates the last few years including possibly the expiring year’s losses) to adjust premium. If the insured had a bad loss year, the mod (and premium) might go up, or the carrier might apply a surcharge. This is when an agent might market the account to see if another carrier will offer a better deal, possibly using _schedule credits or their own view of the risk_. Carriers compete heavily on good risks (e.g. a contracting firm with **improving loss trends** might get a better quote from a new carrier trying to win the business). Conversely, if losses are poor, renewal options might be limited to higher rates or even non-renewal; the agent then may have to move the account to State Fund or a specialty carrier.
    
- **Continuous Improvement:** On renewal, an agent should also revisit **safety and risk management plans** with the client. Demonstrating improvements (like newly instituted training programs or safety gear investments) can be communicated to underwriters for better consideration. While this isn’t a “form field” per se, it can be shared in a cover email or renewal submission narrative.
    
- **Retention of Records:** It’s worth noting that maintaining a thorough record of the insured’s submission data from year to year is helpful. If you wrote the policy originally, you have the ACORD forms and supplements from last year – these can be updated rather than starting from scratch. Using an agency management system, you can often duplicate the prior submission and just modify fields. This ensures consistency year-over-year (which is important because discrepancies in answers from one year to the next will raise questions; e.g. if last year’s app said “0% subcontracted work” and this year says “30%”, be ready to explain the change).
    

In essence, **renewal workflows** are about keeping the data current and accurate, whether staying put or moving carriers. The heavy lift of gathering data is usually at new business inception – after that, it’s about _maintenance and verification_. New business or renewal, the goal is the same: a complete, accurate submission that allows the underwriter to quote quickly and confidently. By focusing on data quality and providing all required forms (ACORD, supplements, loss runs, etc.), a producer increases their chances of securing the best terms for their client and avoids delays or surprises in both new placements and renewals.

## Conclusion

**Submitting a workers’ compensation risk in California – especially a construction or contracting account – requires a disciplined, detail-oriented approach.** The producer must effectively **wear many hats**: form-filler, data analyst, storyteller, and liaison with carriers. Starting with the universal ACORD 125 and 130 forms ensures no fundamental information is missed, as these forms prompt for everything from FEIN to safety plans. From there, gathering **supplemental details** (through carrier questionnaires or your own thorough interview with the client) will cover the nuances that differentiate a mediocre submission from an excellent one.

 

In California, where numerous carriers and programs are available, an independent agent leverages that by preparing one master submission and shopping it around. With the **carrier landscape** ranging from State Fund to private insurers and MGAs, knowing each one’s requirements (e.g. who needs what supplement, who has a portal vs. email) is part of the workflow. Modern tools (comparative raters, upload platforms) can greatly help consolidate this process, but the **core data still comes from the ACORD forms and loss runs** – garbage in, garbage out. Thus, accuracy and thoroughness cannot be overstated.

 

Finally, **loss runs and loss history** form the credibility of the submission. They show whether the insured’s experience backs up the story on the application. A great loss record can be your trump card in negotiations, while a poor one means you must work harder to persuade a carrier (through explanations and safety improvements) to take the chance. In either case, having those documents ready and understanding what they imply (e.g. how they feed into the X-Mod and pricing) is essential for the producer navigating the submission.

 

By diving deep into each grouping of information and understanding how carriers use it, we can appreciate that a workers’ comp submission is not just a paperwork exercise – it’s about **presenting a risk in the best light, with full transparency**. The reward for doing it right is faster quotes, more options, and often better pricing for the client. With this solid picture of the **data landscape and workflow** in mind, an insurance producer can approach California workers’ comp (new business and renewals alike) with confidence and a competitive edge in the marketplace.
