# Workflow of Personal Lines Rating (PL Rater) for Auto and Home Insurance Quotes (CA vs. TX)

**Understanding how an insurance producer uses PL Rating to quote personal auto and homeowners policies requires examining each major section of data they must fill in.** In PL Rating (Vertafore’s personal lines comparative rater), the agent enters client and risk information once and the system returns quotes from multiple carriers. The workflow is organized into logical groupings – typically Applicant/Client info, details of the insured asset (vehicle or property), additional interests (additional drivers or insureds), coverage selections, and prior history (like claims or incidents). Below, we break down these sections in detail for **personal auto** and **homeowners** quotes, highlighting state-specific nuances for **California vs. Texas** and noting how certain answers can change the quoting flow or outcome.

## Personal Auto Insurance – PL Rating Quoting Workflow

When quoting a **personal auto policy** in PL Rating, the agent proceeds through several key data sections: the client and driver information, vehicle details, coverage options, and driver history. PL Rating’s interface reflects this with tabs or pages for each category (e.g. _Client Info_, _Drivers_, _Vehicles_, _Incidents_, _Coverages_). Below is a detailed breakdown of the information needed and how different answers or state rules affect the process.

### Applicant & Driver Information

The first step is entering the **applicant’s personal information and household drivers**. On the PL Rating **Client Information tab**, the agent inputs the primary named insured’s details and can add a spouse or co-applicant as needed. Key data points include:

- **Basic Personal Details:** Full name, date of birth, and contact information of the primary insured (and spouse if applicable). The address is entered here as well (this also determines rating territory). The agent will indicate if the client **owns or rents their home**, since homeownership can earn an auto discount and helps identify multi-policy opportunities. They may also note how long the client has lived at the current address (some carriers value stability of residence).
    
- **Identification Details:** Driver’s license numbers and state of issue for each driver in the household. **Every household member of driving age** typically must be listed – even if someone (like a teen or roommate) will not be a regular driver, they are usually disclosed to carriers. The agent also collects **social security numbers** for the insured (and spouse) in many cases, because several insurers require SSNs to run credit-based insurance scores or verify identity. (In California, credit scores cannot be used for personal insurance rating, per Prop 103, so SSN is less critical for pricing. In Texas, however, most carriers do use credit information, so having an SSN helps produce an accurate quote.)
    
- **Driver Characteristics:** For each driver, PL Rating will capture **gender, marital status, and age** (via DOB), as these impact premiums. **Years licensed** or license issue date can be important too – especially in California, where being licensed ≥3 years is part of qualifying as a “Good Driver” under Prop 103. (California’s Good Driver law mandates a 20% discount for those with 3+ years of licensing and a clean record, and carriers _cannot refuse_ to offer a policy to such drivers. In practice, the agent’s input of the driver’s experience and violation history will determine Good Driver status and discount application automatically in the rating.) In Texas, there is no equivalent law – insurers can choose to decline high-risk drivers – so a short licensing history or many violations might simply yield fewer carrier quotes (or only non-standard carriers).
    
- **Household Composition:** The agent notes the total number of drivers in the household and can designate relationships (e.g. spouse, child) and who should be included or excluded. If the client is married and the spouse drives, the spouse is added as an additional insured driver (marked “co-applicant” if they will be a named insured). **Excluded drivers**: If a household member will **not** be covered (perhaps due to a poor record or other insurance), some states allow naming them as excluded. PL Rating allows the agent to indicate an excluded driver, but doing so may remove some carriers that don’t permit exclusions. (Notably, Hanover’s guidelines mention that having an excluded operator would make a quote ineligible for their elite program.)
    
- **Additional Driver Details:** Modern rating inputs often ask for each driver’s **occupation and highest education level**, since some carriers offer professional/education discounts or use these factors (where allowed). For example, PL Rating’s integration shows fields for education and occupation (e.g. “Bachelors” degree, “Reporter” occupation). In California, factors like education or occupation **cannot** be used in auto rating (by law), so they don’t affect the quote there, but in Texas some carriers might subtly factor them or offer affinity discounts for certain professions. If a driver is a **student**, the agent will note if they qualify for a **Good Student discount** (typically requires a “B” average or better). The system may have a checkbox for good student status or ask for the student’s grade average. If a youthful driver is away at college, the agent can indicate that as well – many insurers give a discount if a student attends school far from home without a car. For example, an agent will input if a college-age child is living on campus over 100 miles away, which could reduce their rating factor.
    
- **Driver Training and Safety Courses:** The application may ask if drivers have taken any approved **safe driving courses**. This is especially relevant for **senior drivers** in some states (55+ who complete a defensive driving course can get a discount) and for teens who completed driver’s education. The Insurance Information Institute notes that agents will want to know if any driver has completed safe driving courses, as this can lower rates. The agent will check the appropriate boxes in PL Rating if such courses apply.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The information in this section can alter subsequent steps or available discounts. For example, if the client **owns a home**, the agent or rater might automatically apply a multi-policy or homeowner discount on the auto quote. If a driver is **under 25 and a student with good grades**, checking the good student field will apply a credit (often 10-15% off for that driver). In California, if the driver has <3 years of licensing or a major conviction, the system will know they do **not** qualify as a “Good Driver,” which means the quote might come in much higher (or from fewer insurers). Conversely, a 20+ year licensed driver with no incidents will trigger the mandated Good Driver Discount in CA (a 20% rate reduction off the best rate they’d otherwise qualify for). These nuances are handled behind the scenes once the agent inputs the data, but it’s crucial they enter all drivers and details accurately – missing a household teen or failing to note a driving course could lead to an incorrect quote or issues at binding.

### Vehicle Information

Next, the producer inputs **vehicle details** for each car the client needs to insure. PL Rating typically supports multiple vehicles on one quote (Hanover notes up to four vehicles can be quoted in one account via PLRater). For each auto, key fields include:

- **Year, Make, Model, and VIN:** The agent either enters the VIN or selects year/make/model from drop-down menus. Providing the full **VIN** is ideal because the system can decode it to identify the exact vehicle and any factory-installed safety features. (If the VIN isn’t entered up front, the agent will need to complete it later before finalizing the policy.) The make, model, and body style (e.g. 4-door sedan, pickup, etc.) affect the vehicle’s insurance rating symbol. For example, a 2018 Chevrolet Camaro ZL1 would be selected or decoded by VIN, and PL Rating would register its characteristics.
    
- **Vehicle Usage and Mileage:** The quoting form asks how each vehicle is used – commonly classified as **pleasure (personal use)**, **commute to work/school**, or **business** use. If “commute” is selected, the agent will input the average one-way commute distance or mileage to work, and possibly the number of days per week the car is driven to work. This is because shorter commutes generally lower the risk exposure. For instance, if the commute distance is under 3 miles, some companies give a lower rate than for a 20-mile commute. (A few insurers even extend the lowest commute rate up to 5 miles.) The agent will also provide an estimate of **annual miles driven** for each vehicle. Low annual mileage can earn discounts – e.g. many carriers give a rate break if a car is driven **under ~8,000 miles per year**. The rater may explicitly ask for annual mileage or categorize it (such as “<7,500 miles, 7,500-15,000, etc.”).
    
- **Ownership and Garaging:** The application will specify whether the vehicle is owned, financed, or leased. If financed or leased, the lienholder’s name and address might be collected (either now or at binding) so the lender can be listed on the policy. The agent also confirms the garaging location – usually the same as the client’s home address unless noted otherwise (if a vehicle is primarily kept elsewhere, that address would be entered). Additionally, the **parking situation** could be noted (some carriers ask if the car is garaged at night or parked on the street), since garaging can affect risk of theft/vandalism.
    
- **Safety and Anti-Theft Features:** Because many discounts are tied to safety equipment, the agent ensures any features not picked up by the VIN are noted. The Insurance Information Institute advises having information on **passive restraints (airbags)**, **anti-lock brakes**, and **anti-theft devices** for each car. Modern vehicles often have these standard, and VIN decoding typically tells the rater, but if not, the agent may answer yes/no to questions about airbags, ABS, and alarm or tracking devices. For example, a car with a factory alarm or a LoJack GPS tracker may qualify for an anti-theft discount. If the client has installed additional safety tech (like telematics devices or dashcams), those aren’t standard rating factors yet in PL Rating, but insurers’ proprietary systems might give credit – the agent could note it under carrier-specific questions if applicable.
    
- **Assigned Driver:** In multi-driver households, the agent will assign each vehicle a primary driver (the person who drives it most) and list any occasional drivers. PL Rating allows assigning drivers to vehicles so that each vehicle’s rate is primarily based on its primary driver’s characteristics. The system will still consider all household drivers for each vehicle (for potential occasional use), but primary assignments affect how quotes are calculated.
    

**Flow dependencies:** Vehicle info heavily influences which coverages are available and the premiums. For instance, the **age and model of the car** determine if certain coverages make sense – a brand new car or a valuable model will likely include physical damage coverage (comp and collision), whereas an old $1,500 beater might be quoted “liability only.” Some rating systems even prompt or default to liability-only if the car is beyond a certain age or value, though the agent can override that. The **annual mileage and usage** directly feed into rating: a long daily commute or high annual miles will raise the risk (and thus the quotes) compared to a low-mileage pleasure-use vehicle. If “business use” is selected (e.g. a salesperson’s vehicle), some standard carriers might decline to quote or surcharge the rate – the rater might alert the agent that certain companies don’t allow business use on a personal policy. Location matters too: PL Rating uses the ZIP code/garaging address for territory rating. **California vs. Texas**: One difference is that Texas has a high incidence of hail, so insurers there sometimes ask about the vehicle’s parking (garage vs street) to gauge hail exposure, whereas California insurers are more concerned with theft rates in the area. These specifics might not appear as explicit questions in PL Rating, but they manifest in the rate.

**Example dependency:** **Commute distance** – If the agent inputs a very short commute (say 2 miles), some carriers will automatically apply their “short commute” discount or lower rating factor, leading to a noticeably lower quote than if it were 20 miles (some carriers tier it at <3 miles, <5 miles, etc.). **Annual mileage** – entering 7,500 miles/year versus 15,000 can trigger a low-mileage discount (many companies give a discount for under 8K or 10K miles/year). It’s critical the agent estimates this honestly with the client, since an unrealistic low number might cause issues if a claim’s odometer reading tells a different story.

### Coverage Selections and Policy Options (Auto)

Once drivers and vehicles are entered, the producer selects the desired **coverages, limits, and deductibles** for the auto policy. This section in PL Rating usually has default liability limits and options which the agent can adjust to match the client’s needs (often matching their current coverage if it’s a renewal shop). Key coverage choices include:

- **Liability Coverage:** This is the bodily injury (BI) and property damage (PD) liability limits for the policy. The agent will choose split limits (e.g. $100,000 per person / $300,000 per accident for BI, and $50,000 property damage) or a combined single limit (CSL) if available. California and Texas minimum liability limits differ (CA minimum is 15/30/5, TX is 30/60/25), but most clients carry higher limits. In PL Rating, the default might populate at state minimum or a common recommendation (like 50/100/50), which the agent can change. Agents often refer to the client’s asset situation when choosing limits – for instance, Allstate notes an agent may ask about the client’s assets and home ownership **to help determine appropriate liability limits** (protecting those assets). If the client is a homeowner with substantial assets, the agent will likely quote higher limits (250/500/100 or an umbrella) versus a renter with no assets who might choose lower limits.
    
- **Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist (UM/UIM):** The agent selects UM/UIM coverage limits. In many cases, the system defaults these to match the liability limits (e.g. $100k/$300k) unless the client wishes to reject or carry lower limits. **State difference:** Both CA and TX require that UM/UIM be offered. In California, an applicant can reject UM/UIM in writing, otherwise it’s typically included up to the BI limits. In Texas, UM/UIM is similarly offered by default. The PL Rater workflow will include UM coverage selection – the agent can choose to include it or (if the client insists) reject it. Usually, agents quote it included, since it’s good protection.
    
- **Personal Injury Protection (PIP) vs. Medical Payments:** This is a key difference between Texas and California. **Texas** requires insurers to **offer PIP** (Personal Injury Protection) of at least $2,500 per person, and it will be included by default unless the client rejects it in writing. PIP covers no-fault medical and some lost wages. **California**, on the other hand, does not have PIP (no-fault system) – instead, **Med Pay** (medical payments) coverage is optional. Med Pay in CA (and TX, if offered) covers medical expenses but is more limited (no wage loss). In PL Rating, if the quote state is Texas, the form will likely pre-check PIP at $2,500 per person (the minimum) by default. The agent can increase the PIP limit (e.g. TX carriers often offer $5,000 or $10,000 PIP options) or note if the client wants to reject it (which would require a rejection form later). In California, the quote form might default Med Pay to “No coverage” unless the agent selects an amount (commonly $1,000, $5,000, etc.). The OPIC guidance in Texas highlights that PIP $2,500 must be provided unless rejected – so an agent in TX will typically leave it on unless the client is very price-sensitive and opts out (Med Pay is not mandated to be offered in TX, but some carriers provide it as an either/or choice with PIP).
    
- **Comprehensive and Collision:** For each vehicle, the agent indicates whether to include **collision coverage** and **comprehensive (other-than-collision) coverage**, which together constitute “full coverage.” If included, the **deductible amounts** are selected (e.g. $500 collision deductible, $500 comp). PL Rating will have drop-downs for each coverage per vehicle. The deductible choice can be important: choosing a higher deductible (like $1,000 instead of $250) will lower the premium. The agent will typically mirror the client’s current coverage if they have it, or discuss what makes sense given the car’s value. For example, a newer car with a loan will need both comp and collision (the lienholder requires it). An older car might omit collision if the cost isn’t worth it. The rater can quote some vehicles with and some without, depending on input. In Texas and other states prone to hail, sometimes **comprehensive** coverage is especially important – Texas leads the U.S. in hail claims, so comp (which covers hail) is highly recommended. The agent will ensure comp is checked for Texas customers who want protection from hail, wind, theft, etc.
    
- **Additional Coverages:** The rater will include fields for other optional coverages such as:
    
    - **Towing and Labor (Roadside Assistance)** – an inexpensive add-on that reimburses small towing expenses.
        
    - **Rental Reimbursement** – coverage for a rental car if the insured vehicle is in the shop for a covered claim. The agent can select a daily limit (e.g. $30/day for 30 days).
        
    - **Glass coverage:** Some carriers offer **Full Glass** coverage (no deductible for windshield claims). Hanover, for instance, notes that an agent _can select full glass coverage in the rater_ if the client wants it.
        
    - **Loan/Lease Gap coverage:** If the client has a newer financed vehicle, the agent might have the option to add gap coverage to pay off a loan if the car is totaled. Not all carriers’ quotes include this option in PL Rating, but if available, it would be a checkbox under vehicle coverages.
        
    - **Special Equipment** or **Custom parts**: if the vehicle has custom upgrades (rims, sound system, etc.), some raters allow adding a dollar amount of custom equipment coverage.
        
- **Policy Term and Payment:** The agent may also select the policy term (6-month vs 12-month). Many carriers in California use 6-month auto policies, whereas 12-month is common in Texas. PL Rating allows term selection, and some carriers’ quotes might only come back for one term or the other. Additionally, while not always an input, it’s useful to note that PL Rating **assumes full-pay** by default for pricing in many cases – e.g. it “automatically include[s] the Paid in Full discount” in the quote for most states. This means the premium shown is a lump-sum rate; other payment plans might have slightly higher rates or fees. Agents can usually see other billing options on carrier sites, but the comparative quote is typically the best-case (full pay) scenario.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The coverages selected can affect which carriers will quote or what additional questions appear. For example, if the agent tries to select very high liability limits (say $500,000 CSL), some non-standard carriers that only go up to $100k will simply not return a quote. If the client wants **non-owners** insurance (a policy for someone who doesn’t own a car but occasionally drives others’ cars), PL Rating now has a feature for that – the agent would indicate a **Non-Owner policy** quote, which removes vehicle info and only allows liability coverage. Only carriers that offer non-owner policies would respond. Similarly, if an **SR-22 filing** is needed (for someone who must show proof of financial responsibility to the DMV due to infractions), the agent can input that requirement. PL Rating introduced fields to accommodate **financial responsibility filings (SR-22)** in auto quotes. Checking that a driver “needs SR-22” will filter to carriers that provide it and will add the state filing fee into the quote.

Another example: In Texas, because **PIP must be offered**, the quoting flow will include PIP by default. If the client absolutely doesn’t want PIP, the agent might have to uncheck it (if allowed in the rater) or know that they’ll sign a rejection. Not selecting PIP could slightly reduce the premium, but Texas law requires the default offering of $2,500. In **California**, one interesting dependency is that if a client opts to **reject UM coverage** or select lower UM limits, they must sign a waiver – the system will allow the quote but the agent knows to obtain signed rejection at binding. Generally, most agents quote UM/UIM equal to liability limits unless instructed otherwise, because it’s important coverage.

### Driver Incident / Accident History

A critical part of the auto workflow is documenting the **driving history** for each driver. PL Rating has an **“Incident Information” page** (or section) devoted to listing accidents, violations, and claims in the past X years (usually 3 to 5 years of history are considered). This is often a separate step after entering drivers and vehicles. The agent will ask the client about any tickets, at-fault accidents, and claims. Key aspects of this section:

- **Accidents:** The agent enters any auto accidents the drivers had. They will note the date of each accident, type (collision, etc.), and fault (at-fault vs not-at-fault). Some raters also ask for the amount paid in claims or whether there were injuries, since a minor fender-bender vs a major accident can be rated differently. In California, an at-fault accident that causes over $1,000 damage or any injury counts as 1 point for rating. The agent’s input here will tell the system if the driver loses their good-driver status. Not-at-fault accidents typically don’t surcharge in most states, but they are still often recorded.
    
- **Traffic Violations:** Any moving violations (speeding tickets, red light, etc.) for each driver are listed with offense date and type. These translate to points or surcharges in the rating. Major violations like a DUI are especially important to input as they severely impact rates (and some carriers will decline quoting if a DUI is on record). The agent will usually ask for ~5 years of history on serious violations (since DUI in the past 7 years disqualifies Good Driver status in CA, for instance). Minor tickets typically 3 years.
    
- **Claims/Comprehensive Losses:** Some incidents that are not driving violations still count in pricing. For example, multiple **comprehensive claims** (like auto thefts or glass claims) can lead to surcharges with certain insurers. The agent can record claims such as hail damage, windshield replacements, theft, vandalism, etc., if the client had those. The PL Rating interface may include these under incidents or have a “Claims” subsection. The integration example from Canopy Connect shows an Auto policy claims history including a “05/22/2022 – Accident ($3,832 payout)” and a “03/25/2019 – Windshield claim”. So, even a glass-only claim was captured. If the client has multiple comp claims (like 3 glass claims), some carriers might rate them higher or exclude glass coverage; the agent’s entry of those ensures the quotes are accurate.
    
- **Automation (Prefill):** To streamline this, PL Rating offers an **Incident Prefill** service in many states. If the agent enters the driver’s license numbers up front, the system can ping a database (TransUnion) to retrieve court records of tickets/accidents. The incidents auto-populate on the incident page, and the agent simply verifies them with the client. For example, if John Doe has a 2019 speeding ticket, it might prefill to the PL Rating incident list, saving the agent time. If nothing pre-fills, that usually means the driving record came back clean (or the data couldn’t find anything, which generally implies a clean record). Agents love this feature as it avoids relying purely on the client’s memory and can prevent unintentional omissions.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The driving history significantly alters the quote results. **Clean vs not clean** is a huge factor. If the agent enters zero incidents for a driver, they’ll qualify for incident-free or “good driver” discounts from carriers, and in California the Prop 103 Good Driver 20% discount applies if other criteria met. If even one at-fault accident is entered, the quotes will jump higher and some preferred carriers might drop out. For example, certain preferred carriers may only quote drivers with 0-1 minor violations; if a DUI or multiple at-fault accidents are input, those carriers will return “no quote” and the agent will see only non-standard carriers’ rates (which are higher). In Texas, there is more flexibility for insurers to refuse; a driver with a recent DUI might only get quotes from the non-standard market. In California, even a DUI driver _must_ be offered a policy by an insurer of their choice if they meet the 3-year licensing and one-point criteria – but a DUI is 2 points so they fail “Good Driver” status. So practically, a CA DUI driver doesn’t get the mandated discount or protection, and they often end up with high premiums or in the assigned risk plan.

The agent’s input also affects whether **SR-22** is needed – if the client said “I need an SR-22 filing,” the agent flags that and only carriers who file SR-22 will quote (and with the filing fee added). Also, if any driver has an incident that implies an **excluded driver** scenario (e.g. a high-risk household member the client doesn’t want to insure), the agent might choose to exclude them, as mentioned. Excluding a driver will remove that driver’s record from rating, but carriers that don’t allow exclusions will simply not quote in many cases.

**Example dependency:** A concrete example is **California’s Good Driver discount**: if the agent enters no at-fault accidents and no more than one minor ticket in 3 years for a driver who’s been licensed >3 years, the system will categorize them as a Good Driver. All carriers in CA must then apply a **20% discount off their base rate** for that driver. The quotes returned will reflect that discount. If the agent instead enters, say, two speeding tickets, that driver loses Good Driver status – the quotes might be dramatically higher (often 30-50% higher) and some insurers won’t even be in the mix because the driver is now “Tier B” or “C” material. As another example, if the client had an **at-fault accident last year**, the agent’s entry will cause carriers to include an accident surcharge; some carriers might still quote but at a higher rate, and others could decline if the accident was major. If multiple incidents are input (e.g. 1 accident + 2 tickets), expect very few standard carriers to show up – the rater may only return quotes from high-risk specialists. Thus, this section dynamically filters and adjusts pricing from carriers.

### Prior Insurance and Discounts

Finally, the producer will enter details about the insured’s **prior insurance history and other discount-relevant info**. Insurance companies want to know if the customer is coming with continuous coverage and what their prior limits were, as well as any other policies that might give discounts. In PL Rating’s flow, these questions might appear under a _General Information_ or _Prior Insurance_ section (often part of the initial client info page). Key points include:

- **Current or Prior Auto Insurance:** The agent notes whether the client has an existing auto policy and its details. This typically includes:
    
    - **Current insurer name** (especially if the rater is configured to bridge to that carrier, but also for the agent’s knowledge).
        
    - **Number of years continuously insured** (with no lapse). For example, “insured continuously for 5+ years” might yield a better rate class than someone who has a 6-month lapse. Hanover’s Platinum guidelines required “no lapse in past 3 years” for eligibility, which shows how prior insurance stability is crucial for best programs.
        
    - **Prior liability limits** on the current policy. Many carriers tier their rates based on prior limits – a person who carried 100/300 before is seen as more responsible (and gets a better rate) than someone who only carried state minimum. So the agent will input something like “Current BI limits: 50/100” if known. If the client has their **declarations page**, the agent can easily find this info. (The Baily Insurance guide suggests having your current policy on hand for this reason.)
        
    - **Expiration date** of the current policy – this indicates the desired effective date for the new policy (so quotes can be run for that date).
        
- **Lapses or No Prior**: If the client has had a **lapse in coverage**, the agent will indicate that (some raters ask “any lapse in last 30 days?”). No prior insurance at all (e.g. first-time driver or someone who only used public transport before) is also noted. This can significantly affect quotes. In Texas, many standard carriers charge a surcharge or won’t take a customer with no prior continuous insurance. In California, an insurer cannot surcharge for lack of prior per se for a Good Driver (and Prop 103 even allows Good Drivers with no prior to still get the 20% discount). In practice though, having had insurance helps your rate in most places. PL Rating will include this info in the quote request to carriers. If a lapse is indicated, some preferred carriers may decline to quote or rate the client in a higher-risk tier.
    
- **Other Policies / Multi-line Discounts:** The agent will note if the client has other existing policies that could lead to discounts. For example, if the client also needs a homeowners quote (or already has homeowners with the agency), the agent might use PL Rating’s **package quote** feature to quote home and auto together. In a **package quote workflow**, PL Rating collects both auto and home info in one flow and applies multi-policy (“account”) discounts to each line accordingly. If the agent is quoting auto alone but the client has a home policy with another carrier, they might indicate “owns home – currently insured elsewhere” to still get a homeowner discount on the auto (some carriers give a small discount just for homeownership, even if the home isn’t with them). Hanover’s tip sheet mentions “Account Credit defaults to Yes” in carrier-specific questions – meaning if quoting a package, it assumes multi-policy. The agent can deselect it if, say, the client decides not to bundle in the end.
    
- **Discount Qualifications:** Beyond multi-line, the agent ensures any miscellaneous discounts are captured:
    
    - **Paid-in-Full** – as noted, PL Rating auto quotes often assume full payment discount is taken. If the client knows they must pay in installments, the agent might mentally note the premium could be slightly higher.
        
    - **Good Student, Defensive Driver** – covered earlier in driver info.
        
    - **Anti-theft, safety devices** – covered in vehicle info.
        
    - **Affinity/Occupation** – if the client is e.g. a member of an alumni association or a particular occupation that some carriers discount (military, teacher, engineer, etc.), the agent might have to add that under specific carrier questions. This is often outside the core comparative rating input, unless the rater has generic fields for “occupation” which we discussed. In Texas, carriers commonly use an insurance credit score which is automatically applied (the client doesn’t see it, but a good credit score can yield a significant discount). In California, that factor is absent, so other factors like driving record carry more weight.
        

**Flow dependencies:** Indicating **continuous prior insurance** generally results in lower quotes. For example, if the agent marks that the client has had insurance continuously for 3+ years with no lapse, most carriers will provide their best rate tier (assuming driving record is clean). If instead “no prior insurance” is selected, some carriers in TX will either not quote or quote at a much higher premium (viewing the client as higher risk). The comparative rater will automatically factor this into the rates returned. Similarly, if the agent is running a **bundled auto/home quote** in PL Rating, the presence of the bundle triggers multi-line discounts on both the auto and home quotes. The user of PL Rating just enters the data for both policies and links them as a package; the software then shows the discounted package premiums. If the agent were to quote them separately without indicating a bundle, the premiums might each be higher.

**Example dependency:** If a client had **no prior insurance for the last year**, an insurer like Progressive or GEICO (through the rater) might still quote but at their “Non-standard” program rates, which could be, say, 20% higher than if the client had continuous coverage. On the rater’s interface, the agent might get a warning or just see higher premiums. Conversely, a client with **15 years with State Farm and no lapse** will get the best “persistency” or prior insurance discounts from many carriers, yielding lower quotes. Another example: marking “Currently have a homeowners policy with XYZ” can give a typical auto discount of perhaps 5-10%. If using PL Rating’s combined quote feature, that “account credit” is automatically applied (the Hanover PL Rating tips explicitly note that in package quoting, the system gives the account credit by default). If for some reason the client opts not to actually bundle at purchase, the agent would have to remove that credit (which would raise the premium).

At this stage, the agent has filled in all necessary fields. They would then click **Rate** or **Submit** in PL Rating, and the system will transmit this data to the selected insurance carriers’ rating engines and return quote premiums. The agent can then review the **Quote Results** screen, comparing prices and coverage variations. They might adjust some inputs (coverage limits, deductibles, etc.) and re-rate to fine-tune quotes or satisfy any carrier-specific missing info. For instance, a carrier might return a message “Roof age required for Home quote” or “Driver’s license number invalid format” – the agent would fix these, then re-run as needed.

## Homeowners Insurance – PL Rating Quoting Workflow

Quoting a **homeowners (HO) policy** in PL Rating follows a similar structured approach, with sections for applicant info, property details, coverages, and prior loss history. Home insurance requires more detailed property information (construction, features, etc.) than auto, so the data entry is extensive. The agent’s goal is to provide a complete picture of the home’s characteristics so that carriers can accurately calculate a dwelling replacement cost and premium. We will break down the process and highlight how answers (e.g. age of roof) can change outcomes, as well as differences in **California vs. Texas** home insurance scenarios.

### Applicant and Property Location Information

First, the agent enters the **client (applicant) information and property address** for the home policy. If this quote is part of a bundle with auto, some of the basic info may carry over. Key pieces here:

- **Named Insured(s) and Contact Info:** The homeowner’s name, and usually a second named insured if applicable (often a spouse or domestic partner), go on the application. In PL Rating’s Client Info, the agent can add a co-applicant similar to auto. The address of the property to be insured is entered – if the mailing address is different, that can be specified too. It’s important to get the ZIP code correct as it ties to fire risk, territory rates, etc.
    
- **Occupancy and Use of Home:** The agent specifies how the home is occupied: primary residence, secondary (vacation) home, seasonal use, or tenant-occupied (rental). A standard Homeowners (HO3) policy is for owner-occupied primary residences; secondary/seasonal homes can often be written on an HO3 as well, but some carriers treat them differently. The rater will have an occupancy field. If “rental property” or “tenant occupied” were chosen, the system might actually require switching to a dwelling fire policy quote instead of homeowners (since HO3 is owner-occupied). So generally for a homeowners quote, the agent will mark it as a primary residence (or secondary if the client indeed has a second home). They will also indicate if the insured is the owner of the home (nearly always yes for HO).
    
- **Additional Interests:** If the client has a **mortgage** on the home, the agent may note that a lender will be added. While the lender’s name/address might not be needed for just a quote, knowing there’s a mortgage reminds the agent that the policy will need to include that interest (and sometimes carriers give a slight discount for mortgages because lenders enforce home maintenance – this is minor if at all). Travelers asks “Do you have a mortgage on the home?” as part of insurance history questions, likely just to prompt the appropriate follow-up.
    
- **Household Residents:** The quoting form might ask who lives in the house. The agent will confirm if the insured lives there full-time, and if there are any other occupants (like relatives, tenants, etc.). Number of residents isn’t usually a rating factor, but **presence of any business or roomers** is important. **Home business use:** The agent must indicate if the client conducts any business from the home (like a day care, a home office with client visits, etc.). Many carriers will still insure a home with an office exposure (often requiring a home business endorsement), but some activities (running a daycare or storefront) can make a home ineligible for a homeowners policy. PL Rating will include a question about business use: e.g. “Any business conducted on premises?” If “yes,” it might require details or automatically limit which carriers quote.
    
- **Pets – especially Dogs:** The agent will ask if the client has any dogs (or other potentially liability-raising pets like exotic animals). **Dog ownership** is a common underwriting question because certain breeds with aggressive reputations can cause carriers to decline coverage or exclude dog liability. Travelers’ checklist explicitly mentions the quote process may ask **whether you own a dog or any other pets**. If the client answers yes, some carriers will then require the breed or size. PL Rating might not capture breed specifics in the initial quote, but the agent may jot it down for later because if it’s a **pit bull, Rottweiler, etc.,** a number of standard insurers in both CA and TX will not insure without an exclusion (or at all). At minimum, having a dog increases liability risk – Plymouth Rock notes that owning certain breeds can affect the cost due to increased liability exposure. The agent’s “yes” on dog ownership might trigger an alert or just be relayed to carriers, some of which might respond with higher quotes or refer conditions.
    
- **Marital Status and Household Info:** Marital status is usually already known from the auto policy if bundled, but if quoting standalone home, the agent will note if the insured is married (often the spouse should be a named insured if they have ownership). Also, if the insured is retired or over a certain age, some carriers have a retiree discount for home policies, so that could come up (e.g. a question “Is the head of household age 55+ and retired?”).
    

**Flow dependencies:** Basic occupancy answers can change which policy forms or carriers apply. For example, if the agent accidentally indicated “tenant-occupied,” the rater might switch to a dwelling fire quote (different workflow) – so they must ensure it’s correctly set to owner-occupied for a homeowners quote. If **business use** is answered yes (say the client works from home or runs a small side business at home), some carriers may drop off. A minor home office (computer work, no clients coming over) is usually fine, but something like “I bake cakes at home and customers pick up” might trigger eligibility issues. The agent might then know to exclude certain carriers or prepare to add a business endorsement later. **Dog ownership** won’t typically change the premium in the quote phase (unless a carrier outright declines), but it’s noted for underwriting. However, a large aggressive breed could cause some carriers’ quotes to not appear if they simply won’t insure that risk – the agent might not even realize why unless it’s flagged. They may have to address that in underwriting after quoting.

**Example dependency:** If the client says **“I operate a daycare in my home for 5 children,”** and the agent indicates business on premises = yes (and perhaps specifies daycare in a free-form note), most standard carriers will **refuse to quote a homeowners policy** (daycare is a high liability exposure). The agent would likely get zero results from preferred carriers in PL Rating in that case, or a note saying risk ineligible. That is an extreme case. On the other hand, if the client simply has a **home office (remote employee)**, marking “business use: yes” but describing it as office work might still allow quotes, though the agent might need to add a home business endorsement at binding.

Also, consider **California vs. Texas**: not much difference in this basic info section, except perhaps **marital status** trends (no rating difference) and **household composition**. One nuance: California insurers might ask if any occupants are smokers (fire risk), though it’s not as common nowadays to rate on that – it could be a carrier-specific question. Texas doesn’t differ on that front.

### Home Construction and Property Details

This is the most detailed part: describing the dwelling’s construction, age, size, and features. The agent essentially provides data for a **replacement cost estimator (RCE)** to calculate Coverage A (dwelling coverage) and for underwriting to assess risk factors. PL Rating’s interface will have a series of fields covering the following:

- **Year Built:** The year the home was originally built is fundamental. It sets the **age of the home**, which affects everything from base rates to required updates. For instance, a 1900-built home vs a 2015-built home will have different risk considerations. Insurers often have cutoffs for older homes needing updates (e.g. wiring, roofing). The year is entered and if it’s beyond a certain age (often 25-30+ years old), additional questions appear about updates (see below).
    
- **Construction Type:** Whether the house is **frame construction (wood)**, **masonry (brick)**, or other (stucco on frame, log, etc.). Many forms break this into **Construction Type** and **Exterior Walls**. For example, PL Rating might have “Frame” or “Joisted Masonry” as construction type, and a separate field for exterior siding material. The Canopy data shows _Frame Type: Wood_ and _Wall Type: Brick_ for a sample home. This indicates the house has brick veneer over a wood frame. If it were solid masonry, wall type might be “Masonry” and frame type “N/A.” These details affect fire resistance and replacement cost. In California, many homes are wood frame with stucco or siding; in Texas, you also have brick veneer homes – each has slight rating differences (brick is often a tad cheaper to insure because of better fire resistance).
    
- **Home Style and Stories:** The form may ask the **style of home** (colonial, ranch, split-level, etc.) or at least whether it’s 1-story, 2-story, etc. Style and number of stories affect rebuild cost (two-story homes cost a bit more per square foot sometimes). The Travelers checklist notes questions on **the style of the home and exterior finish**. The Plymouth Rock list asks for **number of stories** and total **square footage**. The agent will input the square footage of the living area, as that’s crucial for replacement cost. They’ll also input the number of **families/units** if it’s a multi-family (most HO policies are single-family, but duplex (2-unit) or tri-plex could be an HO3 if owner occupies one unit – some carriers allow that). The data snippet showed _Units: 1_ for a single-family home.
    
- **Square Footage & Rooms:** Total square footage of the house (above grade) is entered. Additionally, details like number of **bedrooms, bathrooms (full/half)**, maybe number of **fireplaces**, and any special rooms (finished basement, etc.) are collected. These are used to refine the replacement cost estimate. For example, Travelers mentions the quote will likely ask for the number of bathrooms and fireplaces. If the home has a finished basement or other notable features (like high-end kitchen, etc.), some systems include questions on those in an “upgrades” section.
    
- **Roof Details:** The type of roof is critical. The agent inputs the **roof material** (asphalt shingles, tile, metal, wood shake, etc.) and possibly the **roof shape** (gable, hip, flat). The Canopy data shows _Roof Cover: Single_ (likely an abbreviation or typo for “Shingle”) and _Roof Shape: Pyramid_ (a type of hip roof). Roof shape matters because a hip roof is more wind-resistant than a gable roof – insurers often give a discount in hurricane-prone areas for hip roofs. The **age of the roof** is extremely important too. The agent will either input the year the roof was last replaced or the roof’s age in years. Carriers care if a roof is new, since a **newer roof can significantly lower premiums** (due to less risk of leaks and better wind resistance). For example, replacing a roof can reduce premiums by **10–20% or more** in some cases. Many companies give a “new roof” discount if the roof is under a certain age (often 5 or 10 years). So if the client says “we put on a new roof 2 years ago,” the agent will input that – yielding a discount. Conversely, an **older roof** can trigger issues: insurers may mandate actual cash value coverage or decline risks when roofs get past 15-20 years without replacement. We’ll discuss that in the context of Texas wind/hail in a moment.
    
- **Update Questions (Wiring, Plumbing, etc.):** If the home is older (typically >25-30 years old), the application will ask if major systems have been updated:
    
    - **Electrical system:** Does the home have circuit breakers or old fuse box? Any aluminum wiring or knob-and-tube? If the wiring was updated, what year?
        
    - **Plumbing system:** Are there any old pipes (galvanized steel, for example) or has it been updated to copper/PVC and in what year? How old is the water heater? Travelers specifically notes questions like _“How old is your water heater? When did you last do major plumbing work?”_.
        
    - **Heating & AC:** What type of heating (gas furnace, electric, etc.) and was the furnace/HVAC updated and when? Is there central air conditioning? (Presence of **central HVAC** can impact cost – homes with central heat and air are viewed as standard, whereas a home with say wood stoves might be a fire hazard).
        
    - **Roof update:** (As already covered – year roof replaced is usually one of the update questions if original roof is old).
        
    
    These update questions allow carriers to gauge if an older home has been modernized. For example, a 1940 home fully updated in 2015 is a much better risk than one still with 1940s wiring and plumbing. If updates are not done and the home is very old, some insurers in both CA and TX might only offer a limited policy or decline. The agent collects as much info on updates as possible. Allstate notes these types of questions about renovations: _“Have you replaced the roof or done major renovations? How recently?”_ and _“How old is plumbing? Custom remodels?”_. This is to ensure the coverage reflects the current home’s value and condition.
    
- **Construction Features:** Additional details often include:
    
    - **Foundation type:** slab, crawlspace, basement. (Basements add cost in RCEs, and in TX many homes are slab-on-grade versus in CA you see more slab or crawl but few basements due to quake risk).
        
    - **Garage:** Attached vs detached, and how many car capacity. E.g. “Garage: attached 2-car, 400 sq ft.” The data snippet had _Garage Type: Attached, 1-Car_ and _Garage Sq Ft: 240_.
        
    - **Porches, decks:** Some forms will ask total square footage of porches or decks, or at least if there is a porch/deck, as it adds to replacement cost.
        
    - **Interior details:** High-level indicators like floor material (hardwood, carpet?), any custom features (vaulted ceilings, etc.) may be asked if using a detailed RCE, but in a quick quote the agent might skip that level. However, **number of kitchens** (1 or 2 if multi-family) and whether there’s a **fireplace** (yes, how many) are commonly asked. The Travelers checklist explicitly lists fireplaces as a detail to gather.
        
    - **Safety systems:** Smoke detectors, fire extinguishers, home security system, fire sprinkler system. These are usually asked because they can earn discounts. Travelers asks if you have safety devices or _smart home technology, security system, fire sprinklers_ – which may qualify for discounts. The agent will check off things like “central station burglar alarm” or “fire alarm monitored” if the client has them, which many carriers give a credit for (often ~5% discount for centrally monitored alarms).
        
    - **Swimming Pool or Trampoline:** Many home apps ask if there is a swimming pool on premises, and if yes, is it fenced and does it have a diving board or slide. Pools increase liability risk, so carriers want to know. Likewise, a trampoline on the property is a liability concern. The Plymouth Rock mention of “lifestyle choices” specifically calls out **having a swimming pool** or certain dog breeds can increase liability risk (and thus premiums). So an agent will usually note if a pool is present. If yes, they’ll later ensure the client has adequate liability coverage and maybe an umbrella. Some carriers might not quote homes with an unfenced pool. PL Rating likely includes “Pool: yes/no” and if yes maybe a follow-up about fencing.
        
- **Fire Protection Class:** A crucial factor for home insurance is how close the home is to fire-fighting resources. The agent will usually input:
    
    - **Distance to nearest fire hydrant** (in feet, or <1000 feet, etc.).
        
    - **Distance to fire station** (in miles). Many carriers require that the home be within e.g. 5 miles of a responding fire station and within 1000 feet of a hydrant to qualify for preferred rates. If it’s further, the Protection Class goes up (worse) and the premium surges or some carriers won’t write it. Plymouth Rock lists _“Distance to fire station”_ as a key rating factor and explains that a home 1 mile from a station will likely cost less to insure than one 15 miles away. The agent must input these distances. If the client isn’t sure, the agent might use mapping tools. In PL Rating, entering these distances helps determine the **Fire Protection Class (ISO PPC)** which underpins the base rate. California brush areas often have higher protection classes due to limited fire resources.
        
    - Additionally, whether the responding fire department is **paid or volunteer** can be a factor (volunteer departments might mean slower response).
        
    - **Hydrant**: If no hydrant within say 1000 feet, that’s a problem for many standard carriers. The agent would note if none is close (some forms ask “>1000 ft?” yes/no).
        

**Flow dependencies:** The property details directly determine the **Coverage A (Dwelling) replacement cost** that PL Rating calculates or the agent inputs. Many comparative raters have a built-in replacement cost calculator that uses all the above info (square footage, construction type, etc.) to estimate rebuilding cost. For example, after inputting data, PL Rating might suggest that the home’s replacement cost is $300,000, and it will pre-fill Coverage A = $300,000 (though the agent can tweak it if they have their own estimate or if the client wants a specific amount). If the agent enters a **very low Coverage A** that seems inconsistent with the data (say the home details imply $500k but agent put $300k), some carriers may not quote or will insure only up to a certain percentage – insurers want proper insurance-to-value. Conversely, if the agent demands a very high Coverage A (beyond what the data suggests), the quotes may be higher and could trigger a re-evaluation by underwriters.

Certain answers can **exclude carriers or require special policies**. For instance:

- If **roof material** is wood shake (high fire hazard) in a California wildfire zone, many standard carriers will not quote. The agent might then only see quotes from surplus line or the FAIR Plan scenario (which PL Rating typically doesn’t handle – FAIR Plan is a last resort market for fire coverage). Recently, carriers also ask about _wildfire mitigation_ (like clearing brush, fire-resistant materials) in CA due to new regulations, but that might be outside the PL Rating standard fields and more in carrier-specific questions.
    
- **Roof age dependency:** This is a big one. If the roof is beyond a certain age, some carriers will either **apply an ACV roof endorsement** or **not quote at all** until it’s replaced. In Texas especially, after severe hail claims in recent years, many insurers now default to **Actual Cash Value (ACV) coverage for roofs older than 10-15 years**. For example, a Texas insurer might quote a 20-year-old roof only with ACV (meaning depreciated value in a claim) or require a higher wind/hail deductible. The agent should be aware: _once a roof passes about 15 years in TX, expect premium increases or coverage limitations_. Indeed, as one Texas agency notes, _“Once your roof is over 10–15 years old, many Texas insurers will increase your premium, require ACV instead of replacement cost, or even deny roof coverage unless it’s replaced. Roofs over 20 years old often require proof of good condition or face non-renewal.”_. In PL Rating, the agent’s input of roof age will influence carriers: some may still quote replacement cost but at a higher rate, others might only offer a policy with an ACV roof clause (which might come through as a note or endorsement on the quote). If the roof was **just replaced (age 0-5 years)**, the agent will definitely indicate that, yielding new roof credits that reduce the premium (often a sizable discount).
    
- **Home age and updates:** If the home was built in, say, 1920 and the agent indicates **no updates** to wiring or plumbing, a lot of carriers will flat-out decline (due to potential for old knob-and-tube wiring or old pipes). The rater might then show quotes only from specialty or high-risk markets or might require the agent to change something (like if possible, “Fuse box: updated to breakers in 1990” – if the client knows any partial updates, it helps). Conversely, indicating **system updates** (e.g. “Electrical updated in 2018, Plumbing in 2010, Roof in 2019”) will **broaden the market of carriers** willing to quote and often lower premiums because the house is less likely to have fire or water issues.
    
- **Protection features:** If the agent checks “central fire alarm and sprinkler system,” some carriers will apply credits. If they check “no smoke detectors,” that could even be an issue (most carriers require at least basic smoke alarms operational). Usually, clients have those, so “yes” is expected.
    
- **Fire station distance:** If the agent inputs “10 miles to fire station” and “no hydrant nearby,” many standard insurers (especially in brush or rural areas of CA) won’t quote, as that’s a Protection Class 9 or 10 scenario. In Texas, rural ranch properties similarly could be tough. PL Rating will still try to return quotes, but they might be extremely high or from surplus markets. On the flip side, a home within **1 mile of a fire station and 500 feet of a hydrant** will get the best Protection Class (e.g. Class 1 in some areas) leading to lower rates.
    

**Example dependencies:** A clear example is **roof age in Texas**. Suppose an agent inputs that the roof was last replaced 20 years ago on a Texas home. Many carriers will interpret that as a substantial risk for hail/wind damage. As a result, the quotes might come back with an **ACV roof endorsement** (meaning any roof claim will be depreciated) or a **higher wind/hail deductible** (like 2% of dwelling value) by default. Some carriers may not quote at all until that roof is replaced. If the agent instead could say the roof was replaced, even partially, in the last 5-10 years, it would keep more carriers in play and yield better rates.

Another example: **Square footage and features affecting replacement cost.** If the agent undershoots the square footage or doesn’t mention a finished basement, the calculated Coverage A might be too low. This could slip through initially, but at binding or inspection the carrier would adjust it (possibly increasing premium). It’s best practice for the agent to be thorough: e.g., if the client finished their basement or added an addition, include that. Travelers’ guide asks specifically about upgrades like a **finished basement or kitchen remodel** because it raises the home’s replacement value. By capturing that (e.g., “Basement: finished, 800 sq ft”), the rater increases the replacement cost and thus the Coverage A. It might make the quote higher, but it ensures proper coverage. If the agent left it out, the quote might look cheap but the coverage would be insufficient.

### Coverage Selection (Homeowners)

After detailing the property, the agent will set the desired **coverage limits and endorsements** for the homeowners policy. This includes the dwelling coverage amount and the associated coverages B, C, D, as well as liability, medical payments, and deductibles. In PL Rating, this is usually the **Coverage Options** or **Policy Coverages** section. Key coverages:

- **Dwelling Coverage (Coverage A):** This is the amount of insurance on the house itself. Often, as mentioned, this will be calculated based on the replacement cost estimator. The agent can also input a specific Coverage A if the client has a figure in mind (like from another RCE or insurance valuation). Ideally, Coverage A should equal the cost to rebuild the home at today’s costs. The quote will heavily depend on this – it’s essentially the insured value. If the agent feels the RCE number is too low/high, they might adjust it and perhaps note an endorsement like **extended replacement cost**. In CA, many policies include **Extended Replacement Cost** (like +25% or +50% additional coverage if costs surge). The agent can select an endorsement for 125% or 150% of Coverage A if the client wants that extra buffer (some carriers include it in certain packages by default). In Texas, extended replacement isn’t as common (some offer it), but with inflation these days it’s more in use everywhere.
    
- **Other Structures (Coverage B):** Typically 10% of Coverage A by default for detached structures (shed, fence, etc.). The rater will either automatically set this (most carriers fix it at 10% on a standard HO3, though some allow adjusting higher). If the client has significant other structures (say a big detached garage or barn), the agent might increase Coverage B. There will be a field for that percentage or amount.
    
- **Personal Property (Coverage C):** Personal property (contents) is usually a percentage of Coverage A (50%–75% standard). Many homeowners policies default to 50% of dwelling for contents for owner-occupied homes, though some carriers default to 70%. PL Rating will likely default Coverage C = 50% of A or allow the agent to set the percentage. If the client has a lot of personal property or prefers a higher amount, the agent can raise it. In CA, some high-value carriers might use higher percentages. The agent will also consider if the client wants **Replacement Cost on Contents** (most do; it might be an option to add if not automatic).
    
- **Loss of Use (Coverage D):** This covers additional living expenses if the home is uninhabitable after a claim. Typically 20% of Coverage A is common. The rater will either default it or let the agent set it. It’s often not something the client changes – it’s proportionate.
    
- **Personal Liability:** The agent selects a liability limit (this covers bodily injury or property damage to others, e.g. someone suing after getting hurt on your property). Common options are $100,000, $300,000, $500,000. Many agents default to **$300,000 liability** nowadays as it’s only a few dollars more than $100k in most cases. If the client has a dog, pool, or high net worth, the agent will definitely recommend $300k or $500k. Umbrella policies start at $300k underlying usually. So PL Rating might default to $300k; the agent can adjust as needed. _State nuance:_ There’s no difference between CA and TX law here, but anecdotally, due to litigation climates one might consider higher limits in CA. In Texas, with higher incidence of dog bite claims and swimming pools (lots of pools in the Sunbelt), high liability is likewise recommended. The Plymouth Rock “lifestyle” mention underscores that **having a pool or certain dog can increase your liability risk** – i.e. you’d want higher liability coverage in such cases. Carriers might not force that, but an agent will advise it.
    
- **Medical Payments:** Typically a small coverage for guest minor injuries. Options might be $1,000, $5,000, or $10,000. The agent will pick one (often $5k is common). This doesn’t hugely impact premium so it’s usually set at a reasonably high level for goodwill.
    
- **Deductibles:** The agent sets the base policy deductible (which applies to perils like fire, water damage, theft, etc.). Common choices: $1,000; some clients opt for $2,500 or higher to save money. A higher deductible lowers premium. In PL Rating, the default might be $1,000. If the client wants to see a lower premium, the agent might also try $2,500 or $5,000 and note the difference. In **Texas**, importantly, there is often a separate **Wind/Hail deductible** (especially in coastal or hail-prone areas). In many cases it’s expressed as a percentage of Coverage A (1% or 2% are common). The agent will specify that if applicable. For example, a Texas policy might have a 1% wind/hail deductible and a $1,000 “all other perils” deductible. In PL Rating’s coverage options, there could be fields to set a separate wind deductible. In **California**, separate wind deductibles are not typical (except earthquake, which is separate coverage). But California does sometimes have separate wildfire deductibles emerging, though still rare; at quoting time usually just one deductible for all perils (other than optional earthquake).
    
- **Endorsements and Additional Coverages:** The agent can add optional coverages:
    
    - **Water Backup** (sewer/drain backup coverage): Very commonly added endorsement, usually offers $5,000 or $10,000 coverage for basement/sewer backups. If the client wants it, the agent selects it in the rater’s optional coverages list.
        
    - **Extended Replacement Cost**: as mentioned, e.g. +25% or +50% on dwelling. Carriers like Safeco, etc., often have this.
        
    - **Scheduled Personal Property**: If the client has high-value jewelry, art, etc., the agent might need to get those items appraised and schedule them. Comparative raters usually don’t handle scheduling individual items in a quote request due to complexity – that’s often done after selecting a carrier. So the agent would likely not input that here beyond maybe indicating “Yes, will schedule $20k jewelry” in a notes field.
        
    - **Earthquake Coverage:** In California, standard homeowners policies do _not_ include earthquake. Earthquake insurance is offered either via the **California Earthquake Authority (CEA)** or private quake insurers. PL Rating does **not** automatically quote earthquake as part of the HO policy (since it’s separate). The agent would discuss it separately. They might note if the client wants an earthquake quote – but that often requires a different system or going to CEA quoting. In Texas, earthquake is not a big concern (some might offer as an add-on if desired, but rare).
        
    - **Hurricane/Wind exclusions:** In Texas coastal counties, many carriers exclude windstorm coverage from the HO policy (homeowners in those areas must get a separate wind policy through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association or similar). In PL Rating, if the property is in a coastal ZIP (Tier 1 counties along the Gulf), the agent might have to check an option like “Exclude Wind Coverage?” for carriers that do so, or simply those carriers won’t return quotes unless wind is excluded. Some comparative raters have an option to quote _HO3 Wind Excluded_ vs _HO3 including Wind_. The agent would be mindful: if in Galveston or coastal Houston areas, likely they’ll be dealing with wind exclusions. For our purposes, just note that location can cause either included or excluded wind. Carriers that do write wind in those areas might have hefty hurricane deductibles. This is a Texas-specific flow dependency – in CA, wind is always included (the peril to worry about is wildfire, which is covered by fire coverage in HO; if carriers won’t write due to wildfire risk, that’s a declination rather than an excluded peril scenario).
        
    - **Building Ordinance or Law**: Many policies include 10% of Coverage A by default. Sometimes an option to increase to higher percentages (for older homes in CA, might want more ordinance coverage).
        
    - **Equipment Breakdown**: a newer optional add-on (covers HVAC/electrical system breakdown etc. for a small premium). Agent can opt to include if client interested.
        
    - **Identity Theft coverage** or **Service Line coverage**: other small endorsements that could be offered. PL Rating might not explicitly list every minor endorsement, but some do have checkboxes for popular ones like water backup, ID theft.
        
- **Policy Form / Package:** If quoting with certain carriers, they might have package tiers (like a standard vs enhanced homeowners package). For example, Hanover had Select, Select Plus, Prestige packages where various coverages are bundled. In PL Rating, the agent might pick one of those if available in carrier-specific questions. Otherwise, they set the individual coverages manually.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The coverage selections will influence price (obviously higher limits = higher premium) and sometimes **carrier eligibility**. For instance, some high-value carriers might require minimum Coverage A amounts (they won’t quote a low-value home). But more commonly, if a client wants a very high liability limit ($500k) only some carriers might offer that on an HO3 (others cap at $300k and would require an umbrella for more). Usually $500k is widely available though. If an agent doesn’t select water backup, a carrier that automatically includes some might have a higher quote vs one that doesn’t; these differences will be in the details.

For **state differences**: A big one is **wind/hail coverage in Texas**. If the property is near the coast, an agent might have to quote with wind excluded (meaning the premium shown is for fire/theft etc., but not hurricane – the client would then also get a separate wind policy quote). This complicates the workflow because PL Rating’s comparison might show some carriers “with wind” and some “without” – the agent must carefully note that. For inland Texas, wind is included but often with a percentage deductible. In California, **wildfire risk** is the elephant in the room – but that’s handled by carriers either quoting or not quoting. The agent may not have a separate “exclude fire” option (except going to FAIR Plan which is outside normal comparative rating). So the main coverage difference is earthquake is separate (usually not quoted here) and maybe building code upgrade coverage needs are higher in CA due to earthquake retrofitting costs (but that’s an advanced detail).

**Example dependencies:** If the agent chooses a **higher deductible** – say $2,500 instead of $500 – the premium might drop significantly (maybe ~10-15%). The client might want to see that difference. So an agent could run two quote scenarios in PL Rating with different deductibles as a comparison. Another example: adding **water backup coverage** for, say, $50/year more – a client with a basement in Texas might absolutely want it (due to heavy rainstorms). If the agent forgets to add it, the quote will be a bit lower, but the client would be missing coverage. Agents often include it by default if they know it’s important and mention the slight increase.

A **Texas-specific scenario**: Many Texas insurers apply a mandatory **1% wind/hail deductible** on all policies in hail-prone regions (which is most of TX). The agent in PL Rating might see that the quote comes with, for example, a $3,000 wind/hail deductible on a $300k house (1%). If the client wants a flat $1,000 deductible for everything, some carriers might allow that in non-coastal areas, but it could raise the premium or not be available. The rater’s output would include these details, and the agent would explain them.

One more conditional: If the client’s home is in a known flood zone, the agent will remind them that **flood insurance** is separate (and not handled by PL Rating for homeowner quotes). They might not input anything about flood in the rater (except maybe noting if the property is in a flood plain for underwriting). In TX, coastal homes might need flood and wind separate. In CA, certain high flood areas are less common but exist. Again, outside the direct quoting, but part of the workflow discussion the agent has with the client.

### Prior Claims History (Homeowners)

Just as with auto, the agent will provide the **loss history for the home**. Homeowners insurance carriers typically ask for any claims in the past 5 years (some ask 3 years) that the prospective insured has incurred, whether at this property or a previous property. PL Rating’s interface will have a section to input prior claims, usually including date, type of loss, and amount paid. The agent will query the client: “Any home insurance claims in recent years? Fire, water damage, theft, liability claims, etc.” Key points:

- **Losses at the property vs by the insured:** In homeowners, some carriers care if the property itself had certain losses (even if under a prior owner), but generally they look at the claims history of the insured (and any at this address under the insured’s ownership). The agent will list each claim the client had. For example: a $10k water damage claim 2 years ago, or a theft claim 4 years ago. The Canopy Connect example shows a **Homeowner Policy claims history** with entries like _12/18/2021 – Wind/Hail – $5,217 paid_ and _09/23/2019 – Fire – $12,255 paid_. The agent would input those if applicable.
    
- **Cause of loss:** Carriers differentiate between **catastrophe losses** (like wildfire, hurricane, hail – things largely out of homeowner’s control) and **non-catastrophe losses** (like a pipe leak or theft). Too many of either is bad, but multiple non-cat losses are especially red flags (could indicate maintenance issues or fraudulent behavior). For instance, Hanover’s Platinum eligibility required no more than 1 non-catastrophic loss in 5 years. The agent will indicate cause (the rater usually has categories: fire, water, theft, liability, weather, etc.). Plymouth Rock notes that _if you have not filed any claims in recent years, you may get a discount; if you have a history of multiple claims, insurers view the property as more susceptible to damage_. Many carriers indeed offer a **claims-free discount** (often ~5-10%). So a client with zero prior claims will get better pricing than one with even one prior claim.
    
- **Number of claims:** The agent will list them out. If the number is high (e.g. 3 claims in 5 years), a lot of carriers will decline to quote. PL Rating may still produce some quotes (from perhaps more expensive carriers). The agent might prepare the client that their options are limited.
    
- **Loss details:** If one of the losses was large or involved certain hazards (like a prior **water loss** is a big deal, since a home that had a water leak could be prone to mold or second leak), carriers may exclude or surcharge for water. The agent can’t directly show that in the rater beyond noting the claim, but the carrier responses might include an advisory or simply a higher rate. A prior **liability claim** (like someone got injured on your property and you were sued) would also scare some insurers. But those are less common.
    

**Flow dependencies:** Inputting prior claims will remove any “claims-free” discount in the quotes. For example, if claims-free discount was 10%, that is lost and premium goes up accordingly. Additionally, the presence of certain claims may cause some carriers to not quote. For instance, a home with **two water damage claims in 5 years** – many standard carriers will say no thanks. Or they might quote but with a **water damage exclusion or a much higher water deductible**. The agent may see a note like “Carrier X will offer policy with a water exclusion endorsement due to prior losses.” It then becomes a decision point. A **catastrophic loss** like one wildfire claim might not penalize as much as say two theft claims. But any loss can remove the incident-free discount. Plymouth Rock emphasizes that **the more claims filed over the years, the more an insurer will view the property as a risk and the higher the quote may be**. So a client with multiple past claims will see higher premiums from all quoting carriers.

If the client has **no prior home insurance** (say a first-time homebuyer or previously just a renter), there’s no claims history to report for a home policy (though the insurer might consider if the client had frequent renters insurance claims, but usually they don’t check that deeply in quoting). However, if they owned the home but let it go uninsured for a while (rare, especially if a mortgage exists requiring insurance), that could be a concern but usually the key is claims.

**Example dependency:** If the agent enters **“1 claim in last 5 years – Lightning fire, $20k paid”**, most carriers will still quote, but the premium might be a bit higher and that client won’t get a claims-free discount. If they enter **“3 claims in 3 years”** (perhaps one water, one theft, one hail), many carriers will simply not quote or will quote with surcharges. The rater might return only one or two options, at very high premiums, essentially indicating the client is in a high-risk pool now. Another example: **Texas specific** – Texas gets a lot of wind/hail claims; if the client had, say, two hail claims in the past 5 years, some insurers might be cautious but since hail is usually considered cat loss, they might still insure but with a higher deductible on wind or recommend roof inspection. If the agent notes those, carriers that suffered heavy hail losses might price high. Meanwhile in **California**, if a client had a **wildfire claim**, currently many insurers are pulling out of covering that area again – the rater may have few results for that address regardless of claims because of wildfire zone issues, but a prior fire claim would certainly be a flag.

At this point, the agent has filled in **Applicant info, Property info, Coverages, and Prior claims**. They would then hit the **Rate/Quote** button. PL Rating will connect to the various insurers’ systems and return quotes.

The agent will review the **homeowners quote results** similar to auto. They’ll see premiums and possibly some notes (like certain coverages or conditions). If some carriers require additional info (e.g. maybe one carrier wants the **Fireline score** for wildfire risk – if not provided, they might return a “Refer to Underwriting” instead of a firm quote). The agent might then know the quote isn’t final for those. Typically, the agent picks the best options and prepares to present them to the client, possibly adjusting coverages or re-quoting different scenarios as needed (e.g. “What if we go with a 2% wind deductible, how much do we save?” – they can tweak and rerun).

## Renewals, Endorsements, and Rewrites – Additional Context

Although PL Rating is primarily used for **new business quoting**, it’s helpful to understand how the workflow differs for **policy renewals, mid-term changes (endorsements), or rewriting a policy**:

- **Renewals:** When a policy comes up for renewal (annually or semi-annually for auto), the current insurer will typically provide a renewal offer directly. The agent doesn’t need to re-enter data into PL Rating just to renew with the same company. However, if the client’s renewal premium is high or they want to shop around, the agent can **re-market** the account using PL Rating. In that case, the workflow is essentially the same as new business: the agent would have the updated info (maybe the home’s rebuild cost has changed, or the driver turned 25, etc.) and run new comparative quotes to see if switching carriers makes sense. Often, agents do this every couple of years for clients to ensure competitive rates. If the client is happy and staying put, the renewal is processed via the carrier systems, not through PL Rating. It’s worth noting that after a new policy is written, the renewal will be automatically sent by the carrier, and the client can then decide to accept it or shop. The agent’s role at renewal might be advisory unless shopping is needed.
    
- **Endorsements (Mid-term Changes):** If the client needs to change something on an active policy (add a vehicle, change a roof update, etc.), that is done as a policy endorsement through the carrier or the agency management system, not through PL Rating. **PL Rating doesn’t process endorsements** – it’s a quoting tool, not a policy service tool. For example, if a client **buys a new car**, the agent will gather the new car info (VIN, coverages needed) and contact the carrier to add it to the existing policy. They might use PL Rating in a limited way to **estimate** the difference in premium by quoting the new car scenario, but the actual change is done via carrier portal or AMS download. As Baily Insurance describes, if you call your agent to add a teen driver or a new vehicle, the agent **“will take your information”** (license, DOB for the teen; or vehicle details) and then update your policy accordingly. This is usually quick since all other info is already on file. If the premium change is large, an agent might on occasion requote the whole account with other carriers (e.g. adding a teen made your current insurer double the rate, so they shop for alternatives – effectively that’s a new business quote again via PL Rating). But endorsement processing itself is outside PL Rating.
    
- **Rewrites:** A rewrite is essentially starting a new policy to replace an old one (often due to a lapse or non-renewal). If a policy cancels for non-payment and the client wants coverage again, some carriers allow a “rewrite” within a short window, otherwise it’s treated as new business. In either case, the agent could use PL Rating to get new quotes. If rewriting with the same insurer after a lapse, it might just be done in carrier system. If the carrier non-renewed the policy (common in CA now for wildfire areas), the agent will have to use PL Rating to find a new carrier willing to write the risk. In practice, a rewrite to a new insurer is just a new business quote and policy, so the PL Rating workflow is the same as discussed – the agent ensures all info is up to date (perhaps the roof was replaced since the last policy, etc.) and then quotes anew.
    

One thing to mention is **data pre-fill for renewals or rewrites**: If the agency has an integration, they might export client data from their management system into PL Rating to avoid re-typing. For example, Vertafore’s systems allow bridging existing client info into PL Rating for a quote. The agent then just updates anything new (like a different car or an updated roof age) and runs the quotes. This makes re-shopping at renewal faster.

In summary, **new policy quotes** (for new customers or new lines of business) are where PL Rating is heavily used. For **renewals**, agents use it selectively if shopping the account. And for in-force changes (**endorsements**), PL Rating isn’t used except to quote adding new coverages as a what-if. Instead, endorsements are handled directly with carriers, often in a matter of minutes by inputting the change and getting a premium adjustment.

If it has been a couple years since the last market comparison, agents often suggest re-quoting – _“If it’s been two or more years since we last quoted your policy, it’s a good idea to call and have us do a quote”_, since there may be new discounts or better rates. That implies using PL Rating again to check the market at renewal time.

Each grouping of information (drivers, vehicles, home construction, prior claims, etc.) is crucial to the accuracy of the quote. Small changes in answers can have significant impacts: for example, a **new roof** can yield sizable discounts in homeowners, a **shorter commute** lowers an auto premium, and a **clean driving record** or **claims-free history** unlocks preferred rates. State regulations and risks also shape the process – California’s laws ensure good drivers get discounts, while Texas insurers pay keen attention to hail-proof roofs and credit scores.

Ultimately, PL Rating streamlines the data entry so that once the producer inputs these fields, they can quickly compare carrier offerings and find the best fit for the client’s needs. The workflow we detailed is the backbone of quoting new policies, and understanding each question’s purpose (and the consequences of each answer) allows an agent to navigate the rating process efficiently and accurately.

# Workflow of Personal Lines Rating (PL Rater) for Auto and Home Insurance Quotes (CA vs. TX)

**Understanding how an insurance producer uses PL Rating to quote personal auto and homeowners policies requires examining each major section of data they must fill in.** In PL Rating (Vertafore’s personal lines comparative rater), the agent enters client and risk information once and the system returns quotes from multiple carriers. The workflow is organized into logical groupings – typically Applicant/Client info, details of the insured asset (vehicle or property), additional interests (additional drivers or insureds), coverage selections, and prior history (like claims or incidents). Below, we break down these sections in detail for **personal auto** and **homeowners** quotes, highlighting state-specific nuances for **California vs. Texas** and noting how certain answers can change the quoting flow or outcome.

## Personal Auto Insurance – PL Rating Quoting Workflow

When quoting a **personal auto policy** in PL Rating, the agent proceeds through several key data sections: the client and driver information, vehicle details, coverage options, and driver history. PL Rating’s interface reflects this with tabs or pages for each category (e.g. _Client Info_, _Drivers_, _Vehicles_, _Incidents_, _Coverages_). Below is a detailed breakdown of the information needed and how different answers or state rules affect the process.

### Applicant & Driver Information

The first step is entering the **applicant’s personal information and household drivers**. On the PL Rating **Client Information tab**, the agent inputs the primary named insured’s details and can add a spouse or co-applicant as needed. Key data points include:

- **Basic Personal Details:** Full name, date of birth, and contact information of the primary insured (and spouse if applicable). The address is entered here as well (this also determines rating territory). The agent will indicate if the client **owns or rents their home**, since homeownership can earn an auto discount and helps identify multi-policy opportunities. They may also note how long the client has lived at the current address (some carriers value stability of residence).
    
- **Identification Details:** Driver’s license numbers and state of issue for each driver in the household. **Every household member of driving age** typically must be listed – even if someone (like a teen or roommate) will not be a regular driver, they are usually disclosed to carriers. The agent also collects **social security numbers** for the insured (and spouse) in many cases, because several insurers require SSNs to run credit-based insurance scores or verify identity. (In California, credit scores cannot be used for personal insurance rating, per Prop 103, so SSN is less critical for pricing. In Texas, however, most carriers do use credit information, so having an SSN helps produce an accurate quote.)
    
- **Driver Characteristics:** For each driver, PL Rating will capture **gender, marital status, and age** (via DOB), as these impact premiums. **Years licensed** or license issue date can be important too – especially in California, where being licensed ≥3 years is part of qualifying as a “Good Driver” under Prop 103. (California’s Good Driver law mandates a 20% discount for those with 3+ years of licensing and a clean record, and carriers _cannot refuse_ to offer a policy to such drivers. In practice, the agent’s input of the driver’s experience and violation history will determine Good Driver status and discount application automatically in the rating.) In Texas, there is no equivalent law – insurers can choose to decline high-risk drivers – so a short licensing history or many violations might simply yield fewer carrier quotes (or only non-standard carriers).
    
- **Household Composition:** The agent notes the total number of drivers in the household and can designate relationships (e.g. spouse, child) and who should be included or excluded. If the client is married and the spouse drives, the spouse is added as an additional insured driver (marked “co-applicant” if they will be a named insured). **Excluded drivers**: If a household member will **not** be covered (perhaps due to a poor record or other insurance), some states allow naming them as excluded. PL Rating allows the agent to indicate an excluded driver, but doing so may remove some carriers that don’t permit exclusions. (Notably, Hanover’s guidelines mention that having an excluded operator would make a quote ineligible for their elite program.)
    
- **Additional Driver Details:** Modern rating inputs often ask for each driver’s **occupation and highest education level**, since some carriers offer professional/education discounts or use these factors (where allowed). For example, PL Rating’s integration shows fields for education and occupation (e.g. “Bachelors” degree, “Reporter” occupation). In California, factors like education or occupation **cannot** be used in auto rating (by law), so they don’t affect the quote there, but in Texas some carriers might subtly factor them or offer affinity discounts for certain professions. If a driver is a **student**, the agent will note if they qualify for a **Good Student discount** (typically requires a “B” average or better). The system may have a checkbox for good student status or ask for the student’s grade average. If a youthful driver is away at college, the agent can indicate that as well – many insurers give a discount if a student attends school far from home without a car. For example, an agent will input if a college-age child is living on campus over 100 miles away, which could reduce their rating factor.
    
- **Driver Training and Safety Courses:** The application may ask if drivers have taken any approved **safe driving courses**. This is especially relevant for **senior drivers** in some states (55+ who complete a defensive driving course can get a discount) and for teens who completed driver’s education. The Insurance Information Institute notes that agents will want to know if any driver has completed safe driving courses, as this can lower rates. The agent will check the appropriate boxes in PL Rating if such courses apply.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The information in this section can alter subsequent steps or available discounts. For example, if the client **owns a home**, the agent or rater might automatically apply a multi-policy or homeowner discount on the auto quote. If a driver is **under 25 and a student with good grades**, checking the good student field will apply a credit (often 10-15% off for that driver). In California, if the driver has <3 years of licensing or a major conviction, the system will know they do **not** qualify as a “Good Driver,” which means the quote might come in much higher (or from fewer insurers). Conversely, a 20+ year licensed driver with no incidents will trigger the mandated Good Driver Discount in CA (a 20% rate reduction off the best rate they’d otherwise qualify for). These nuances are handled behind the scenes once the agent inputs the data, but it’s crucial they enter all drivers and details accurately – missing a household teen or failing to note a driving course could lead to an incorrect quote or issues at binding.

### Vehicle Information

Next, the producer inputs **vehicle details** for each car the client needs to insure. PL Rating typically supports multiple vehicles on one quote (Hanover notes up to four vehicles can be quoted in one account via PLRater). For each auto, key fields include:

- **Year, Make, Model, and VIN:** The agent either enters the VIN or selects year/make/model from drop-down menus. Providing the full **VIN** is ideal because the system can decode it to identify the exact vehicle and any factory-installed safety features. (If the VIN isn’t entered up front, the agent will need to complete it later before finalizing the policy.) The make, model, and body style (e.g. 4-door sedan, pickup, etc.) affect the vehicle’s insurance rating symbol. For example, a 2018 Chevrolet Camaro ZL1 would be selected or decoded by VIN, and PL Rating would register its characteristics.
    
- **Vehicle Usage and Mileage:** The quoting form asks how each vehicle is used – commonly classified as **pleasure (personal use)**, **commute to work/school**, or **business** use. If “commute” is selected, the agent will input the average one-way commute distance or mileage to work, and possibly the number of days per week the car is driven to work. This is because shorter commutes generally lower the risk exposure. For instance, if the commute distance is under 3 miles, some companies give a lower rate than for a 20-mile commute. (A few insurers even extend the lowest commute rate up to 5 miles.) The agent will also provide an estimate of **annual miles driven** for each vehicle. Low annual mileage can earn discounts – e.g. many carriers give a rate break if a car is driven **under ~8,000 miles per year**. The rater may explicitly ask for annual mileage or categorize it (such as “<7,500 miles, 7,500-15,000, etc.”).
    
- **Ownership and Garaging:** The application will specify whether the vehicle is owned, financed, or leased. If financed or leased, the lienholder’s name and address might be collected (either now or at binding) so the lender can be listed on the policy. The agent also confirms the garaging location – usually the same as the client’s home address unless noted otherwise (if a vehicle is primarily kept elsewhere, that address would be entered). Additionally, the **parking situation** could be noted (some carriers ask if the car is garaged at night or parked on the street), since garaging can affect risk of theft/vandalism.
    
- **Safety and Anti-Theft Features:** Because many discounts are tied to safety equipment, the agent ensures any features not picked up by the VIN are noted. The Insurance Information Institute advises having information on **passive restraints (airbags)**, **anti-lock brakes**, and **anti-theft devices** for each car. Modern vehicles often have these standard, and VIN decoding typically tells the rater, but if not, the agent may answer yes/no to questions about airbags, ABS, and alarm or tracking devices. For example, a car with a factory alarm or a LoJack GPS tracker may qualify for an anti-theft discount. If the client has installed additional safety tech (like telematics devices or dashcams), those aren’t standard rating factors yet in PL Rating, but insurers’ proprietary systems might give credit – the agent could note it under carrier-specific questions if applicable.
    
- **Assigned Driver:** In multi-driver households, the agent will assign each vehicle a primary driver (the person who drives it most) and list any occasional drivers. PL Rating allows assigning drivers to vehicles so that each vehicle’s rate is primarily based on its primary driver’s characteristics. The system will still consider all household drivers for each vehicle (for potential occasional use), but primary assignments affect how quotes are calculated.
    

**Flow dependencies:** Vehicle info heavily influences which coverages are available and the premiums. For instance, the **age and model of the car** determine if certain coverages make sense – a brand new car or a valuable model will likely include physical damage coverage (comp and collision), whereas an old $1,500 beater might be quoted “liability only.” Some rating systems even prompt or default to liability-only if the car is beyond a certain age or value, though the agent can override that. The **annual mileage and usage** directly feed into rating: a long daily commute or high annual miles will raise the risk (and thus the quotes) compared to a low-mileage pleasure-use vehicle. If “business use” is selected (e.g. a salesperson’s vehicle), some standard carriers might decline to quote or surcharge the rate – the rater might alert the agent that certain companies don’t allow business use on a personal policy. Location matters too: PL Rating uses the ZIP code/garaging address for territory rating. **California vs. Texas**: One difference is that Texas has a high incidence of hail, so insurers there sometimes ask about the vehicle’s parking (garage vs street) to gauge hail exposure, whereas California insurers are more concerned with theft rates in the area. These specifics might not appear as explicit questions in PL Rating, but they manifest in the rate.

**Example dependency:** **Commute distance** – If the agent inputs a very short commute (say 2 miles), some carriers will automatically apply their “short commute” discount or lower rating factor, leading to a noticeably lower quote than if it were 20 miles (some carriers tier it at <3 miles, <5 miles, etc.). **Annual mileage** – entering 7,500 miles/year versus 15,000 can trigger a low-mileage discount (many companies give a discount for under 8K or 10K miles/year). It’s critical the agent estimates this honestly with the client, since an unrealistic low number might cause issues if a claim’s odometer reading tells a different story.

### Coverage Selections and Policy Options (Auto)

Once drivers and vehicles are entered, the producer selects the desired **coverages, limits, and deductibles** for the auto policy. This section in PL Rating usually has default liability limits and options which the agent can adjust to match the client’s needs (often matching their current coverage if it’s a renewal shop). Key coverage choices include:

- **Liability Coverage:** This is the bodily injury (BI) and property damage (PD) liability limits for the policy. The agent will choose split limits (e.g. $100,000 per person / $300,000 per accident for BI, and $50,000 property damage) or a combined single limit (CSL) if available. California and Texas minimum liability limits differ (CA minimum is 15/30/5, TX is 30/60/25), but most clients carry higher limits. In PL Rating, the default might populate at state minimum or a common recommendation (like 50/100/50), which the agent can change. Agents often refer to the client’s asset situation when choosing limits – for instance, Allstate notes an agent may ask about the client’s assets and home ownership **to help determine appropriate liability limits** (protecting those assets). If the client is a homeowner with substantial assets, the agent will likely quote higher limits (250/500/100 or an umbrella) versus a renter with no assets who might choose lower limits.
    
- **Uninsured/Underinsured Motorist (UM/UIM):** The agent selects UM/UIM coverage limits. In many cases, the system defaults these to match the liability limits (e.g. $100k/$300k) unless the client wishes to reject or carry lower limits. **State difference:** Both CA and TX require that UM/UIM be offered. In California, an applicant can reject UM/UIM in writing, otherwise it’s typically included up to the BI limits. In Texas, UM/UIM is similarly offered by default. The PL Rater workflow will include UM coverage selection – the agent can choose to include it or (if the client insists) reject it. Usually, agents quote it included, since it’s good protection.
    
- **Personal Injury Protection (PIP) vs. Medical Payments:** This is a key difference between Texas and California. **Texas** requires insurers to **offer PIP** (Personal Injury Protection) of at least $2,500 per person, and it will be included by default unless the client rejects it in writing. PIP covers no-fault medical and some lost wages. **California**, on the other hand, does not have PIP (no-fault system) – instead, **Med Pay** (medical payments) coverage is optional. Med Pay in CA (and TX, if offered) covers medical expenses but is more limited (no wage loss). In PL Rating, if the quote state is Texas, the form will likely pre-check PIP at $2,500 per person (the minimum) by default. The agent can increase the PIP limit (e.g. TX carriers often offer $5,000 or $10,000 PIP options) or note if the client wants to reject it (which would require a rejection form later). In California, the quote form might default Med Pay to “No coverage” unless the agent selects an amount (commonly $1,000, $5,000, etc.). The OPIC guidance in Texas highlights that PIP $2,500 must be provided unless rejected – so an agent in TX will typically leave it on unless the client is very price-sensitive and opts out (Med Pay is not mandated to be offered in TX, but some carriers provide it as an either/or choice with PIP).
    
- **Comprehensive and Collision:** For each vehicle, the agent indicates whether to include **collision coverage** and **comprehensive (other-than-collision) coverage**, which together constitute “full coverage.” If included, the **deductible amounts** are selected (e.g. $500 collision deductible, $500 comp). PL Rating will have drop-downs for each coverage per vehicle. The deductible choice can be important: choosing a higher deductible (like $1,000 instead of $250) will lower the premium. The agent will typically mirror the client’s current coverage if they have it, or discuss what makes sense given the car’s value. For example, a newer car with a loan will need both comp and collision (the lienholder requires it). An older car might omit collision if the cost isn’t worth it. The rater can quote some vehicles with and some without, depending on input. In Texas and other states prone to hail, sometimes **comprehensive** coverage is especially important – Texas leads the U.S. in hail claims, so comp (which covers hail) is highly recommended. The agent will ensure comp is checked for Texas customers who want protection from hail, wind, theft, etc.
    
- **Additional Coverages:** The rater will include fields for other optional coverages such as:
    
    - **Towing and Labor (Roadside Assistance)** – an inexpensive add-on that reimburses small towing expenses.
        
    - **Rental Reimbursement** – coverage for a rental car if the insured vehicle is in the shop for a covered claim. The agent can select a daily limit (e.g. $30/day for 30 days).
        
    - **Glass coverage:** Some carriers offer **Full Glass** coverage (no deductible for windshield claims). Hanover, for instance, notes that an agent _can select full glass coverage in the rater_ if the client wants it.
        
    - **Loan/Lease Gap coverage:** If the client has a newer financed vehicle, the agent might have the option to add gap coverage to pay off a loan if the car is totaled. Not all carriers’ quotes include this option in PL Rating, but if available, it would be a checkbox under vehicle coverages.
        
    - **Special Equipment** or **Custom parts**: if the vehicle has custom upgrades (rims, sound system, etc.), some raters allow adding a dollar amount of custom equipment coverage.
        
- **Policy Term and Payment:** The agent may also select the policy term (6-month vs 12-month). Many carriers in California use 6-month auto policies, whereas 12-month is common in Texas. PL Rating allows term selection, and some carriers’ quotes might only come back for one term or the other. Additionally, while not always an input, it’s useful to note that PL Rating **assumes full-pay** by default for pricing in many cases – e.g. it “automatically include[s] the Paid in Full discount” in the quote for most states. This means the premium shown is a lump-sum rate; other payment plans might have slightly higher rates or fees. Agents can usually see other billing options on carrier sites, but the comparative quote is typically the best-case (full pay) scenario.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The coverages selected can affect which carriers will quote or what additional questions appear. For example, if the agent tries to select very high liability limits (say $500,000 CSL), some non-standard carriers that only go up to $100k will simply not return a quote. If the client wants **non-owners** insurance (a policy for someone who doesn’t own a car but occasionally drives others’ cars), PL Rating now has a feature for that – the agent would indicate a **Non-Owner policy** quote, which removes vehicle info and only allows liability coverage. Only carriers that offer non-owner policies would respond. Similarly, if an **SR-22 filing** is needed (for someone who must show proof of financial responsibility to the DMV due to infractions), the agent can input that requirement. PL Rating introduced fields to accommodate **financial responsibility filings (SR-22)** in auto quotes. Checking that a driver “needs SR-22” will filter to carriers that provide it and will add the state filing fee into the quote.

Another example: In Texas, because **PIP must be offered**, the quoting flow will include PIP by default. If the client absolutely doesn’t want PIP, the agent might have to uncheck it (if allowed in the rater) or know that they’ll sign a rejection. Not selecting PIP could slightly reduce the premium, but Texas law requires the default offering of $2,500. In **California**, one interesting dependency is that if a client opts to **reject UM coverage** or select lower UM limits, they must sign a waiver – the system will allow the quote but the agent knows to obtain signed rejection at binding. Generally, most agents quote UM/UIM equal to liability limits unless instructed otherwise, because it’s important coverage.

### Driver Incident / Accident History

A critical part of the auto workflow is documenting the **driving history** for each driver. PL Rating has an **“Incident Information” page** (or section) devoted to listing accidents, violations, and claims in the past X years (usually 3 to 5 years of history are considered). This is often a separate step after entering drivers and vehicles. The agent will ask the client about any tickets, at-fault accidents, and claims. Key aspects of this section:

- **Accidents:** The agent enters any auto accidents the drivers had. They will note the date of each accident, type (collision, etc.), and fault (at-fault vs not-at-fault). Some raters also ask for the amount paid in claims or whether there were injuries, since a minor fender-bender vs a major accident can be rated differently. In California, an at-fault accident that causes over $1,000 damage or any injury counts as 1 point for rating. The agent’s input here will tell the system if the driver loses their good-driver status. Not-at-fault accidents typically don’t surcharge in most states, but they are still often recorded.
    
- **Traffic Violations:** Any moving violations (speeding tickets, red light, etc.) for each driver are listed with offense date and type. These translate to points or surcharges in the rating. Major violations like a DUI are especially important to input as they severely impact rates (and some carriers will decline quoting if a DUI is on record). The agent will usually ask for ~5 years of history on serious violations (since DUI in the past 7 years disqualifies Good Driver status in CA, for instance). Minor tickets typically 3 years.
    
- **Claims/Comprehensive Losses:** Some incidents that are not driving violations still count in pricing. For example, multiple **comprehensive claims** (like auto thefts or glass claims) can lead to surcharges with certain insurers. The agent can record claims such as hail damage, windshield replacements, theft, vandalism, etc., if the client had those. The PL Rating interface may include these under incidents or have a “Claims” subsection. The integration example from Canopy Connect shows an Auto policy claims history including a “05/22/2022 – Accident ($3,832 payout)” and a “03/25/2019 – Windshield claim”. So, even a glass-only claim was captured. If the client has multiple comp claims (like 3 glass claims), some carriers might rate them higher or exclude glass coverage; the agent’s entry of those ensures the quotes are accurate.
    
- **Automation (Prefill):** To streamline this, PL Rating offers an **Incident Prefill** service in many states. If the agent enters the driver’s license numbers up front, the system can ping a database (TransUnion) to retrieve court records of tickets/accidents. The incidents auto-populate on the incident page, and the agent simply verifies them with the client. For example, if John Doe has a 2019 speeding ticket, it might prefill to the PL Rating incident list, saving the agent time. If nothing pre-fills, that usually means the driving record came back clean (or the data couldn’t find anything, which generally implies a clean record). Agents love this feature as it avoids relying purely on the client’s memory and can prevent unintentional omissions.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The driving history significantly alters the quote results. **Clean vs not clean** is a huge factor. If the agent enters zero incidents for a driver, they’ll qualify for incident-free or “good driver” discounts from carriers, and in California the Prop 103 Good Driver 20% discount applies if other criteria met. If even one at-fault accident is entered, the quotes will jump higher and some preferred carriers might drop out. For example, certain preferred carriers may only quote drivers with 0-1 minor violations; if a DUI or multiple at-fault accidents are input, those carriers will return “no quote” and the agent will see only non-standard carriers’ rates (which are higher). In Texas, there is more flexibility for insurers to refuse; a driver with a recent DUI might only get quotes from the non-standard market. In California, even a DUI driver _must_ be offered a policy by an insurer of their choice if they meet the 3-year licensing and one-point criteria – but a DUI is 2 points so they fail “Good Driver” status. So practically, a CA DUI driver doesn’t get the mandated discount or protection, and they often end up with high premiums or in the assigned risk plan.

The agent’s input also affects whether **SR-22** is needed – if the client said “I need an SR-22 filing,” the agent flags that and only carriers who file SR-22 will quote (and with the filing fee added). Also, if any driver has an incident that implies an **excluded driver** scenario (e.g. a high-risk household member the client doesn’t want to insure), the agent might choose to exclude them, as mentioned. Excluding a driver will remove that driver’s record from rating, but carriers that don’t allow exclusions will simply not quote in many cases.

**Example dependency:** A concrete example is **California’s Good Driver discount**: if the agent enters no at-fault accidents and no more than one minor ticket in 3 years for a driver who’s been licensed >3 years, the system will categorize them as a Good Driver. All carriers in CA must then apply a **20% discount off their base rate** for that driver. The quotes returned will reflect that discount. If the agent instead enters, say, two speeding tickets, that driver loses Good Driver status – the quotes might be dramatically higher (often 30-50% higher) and some insurers won’t even be in the mix because the driver is now “Tier B” or “C” material. As another example, if the client had an **at-fault accident last year**, the agent’s entry will cause carriers to include an accident surcharge; some carriers might still quote but at a higher rate, and others could decline if the accident was major. If multiple incidents are input (e.g. 1 accident + 2 tickets), expect very few standard carriers to show up – the rater may only return quotes from high-risk specialists. Thus, this section dynamically filters and adjusts pricing from carriers.

### Prior Insurance and Discounts

Finally, the producer will enter details about the insured’s **prior insurance history and other discount-relevant info**. Insurance companies want to know if the customer is coming with continuous coverage and what their prior limits were, as well as any other policies that might give discounts. In PL Rating’s flow, these questions might appear under a _General Information_ or _Prior Insurance_ section (often part of the initial client info page). Key points include:

- **Current or Prior Auto Insurance:** The agent notes whether the client has an existing auto policy and its details. This typically includes:
    
    - **Current insurer name** (especially if the rater is configured to bridge to that carrier, but also for the agent’s knowledge).
        
    - **Number of years continuously insured** (with no lapse). For example, “insured continuously for 5+ years” might yield a better rate class than someone who has a 6-month lapse. Hanover’s Platinum guidelines required “no lapse in past 3 years” for eligibility, which shows how prior insurance stability is crucial for best programs.
        
    - **Prior liability limits** on the current policy. Many carriers tier their rates based on prior limits – a person who carried 100/300 before is seen as more responsible (and gets a better rate) than someone who only carried state minimum. So the agent will input something like “Current BI limits: 50/100” if known. If the client has their **declarations page**, the agent can easily find this info. (The Baily Insurance guide suggests having your current policy on hand for this reason.)
        
    - **Expiration date** of the current policy – this indicates the desired effective date for the new policy (so quotes can be run for that date).
        
- **Lapses or No Prior**: If the client has had a **lapse in coverage**, the agent will indicate that (some raters ask “any lapse in last 30 days?”). No prior insurance at all (e.g. first-time driver or someone who only used public transport before) is also noted. This can significantly affect quotes. In Texas, many standard carriers charge a surcharge or won’t take a customer with no prior continuous insurance. In California, an insurer cannot surcharge for lack of prior per se for a Good Driver (and Prop 103 even allows Good Drivers with no prior to still get the 20% discount). In practice though, having had insurance helps your rate in most places. PL Rating will include this info in the quote request to carriers. If a lapse is indicated, some preferred carriers may decline to quote or rate the client in a higher-risk tier.
    
- **Other Policies / Multi-line Discounts:** The agent will note if the client has other existing policies that could lead to discounts. For example, if the client also needs a homeowners quote (or already has homeowners with the agency), the agent might use PL Rating’s **package quote** feature to quote home and auto together. In a **package quote workflow**, PL Rating collects both auto and home info in one flow and applies multi-policy (“account”) discounts to each line accordingly. If the agent is quoting auto alone but the client has a home policy with another carrier, they might indicate “owns home – currently insured elsewhere” to still get a homeowner discount on the auto (some carriers give a small discount just for homeownership, even if the home isn’t with them). Hanover’s tip sheet mentions “Account Credit defaults to Yes” in carrier-specific questions – meaning if quoting a package, it assumes multi-policy. The agent can deselect it if, say, the client decides not to bundle in the end.
    
- **Discount Qualifications:** Beyond multi-line, the agent ensures any miscellaneous discounts are captured:
    
    - **Paid-in-Full** – as noted, PL Rating auto quotes often assume full payment discount is taken. If the client knows they must pay in installments, the agent might mentally note the premium could be slightly higher.
        
    - **Good Student, Defensive Driver** – covered earlier in driver info.
        
    - **Anti-theft, safety devices** – covered in vehicle info.
        
    - **Affinity/Occupation** – if the client is e.g. a member of an alumni association or a particular occupation that some carriers discount (military, teacher, engineer, etc.), the agent might have to add that under specific carrier questions. This is often outside the core comparative rating input, unless the rater has generic fields for “occupation” which we discussed. In Texas, carriers commonly use an insurance credit score which is automatically applied (the client doesn’t see it, but a good credit score can yield a significant discount). In California, that factor is absent, so other factors like driving record carry more weight.
        

**Flow dependencies:** Indicating **continuous prior insurance** generally results in lower quotes. For example, if the agent marks that the client has had insurance continuously for 3+ years with no lapse, most carriers will provide their best rate tier (assuming driving record is clean). If instead “no prior insurance” is selected, some carriers in TX will either not quote or quote at a much higher premium (viewing the client as higher risk). The comparative rater will automatically factor this into the rates returned. Similarly, if the agent is running a **bundled auto/home quote** in PL Rating, the presence of the bundle triggers multi-line discounts on both the auto and home quotes. The user of PL Rating just enters the data for both policies and links them as a package; the software then shows the discounted package premiums. If the agent were to quote them separately without indicating a bundle, the premiums might each be higher.

**Example dependency:** If a client had **no prior insurance for the last year**, an insurer like Progressive or GEICO (through the rater) might still quote but at their “Non-standard” program rates, which could be, say, 20% higher than if the client had continuous coverage. On the rater’s interface, the agent might get a warning or just see higher premiums. Conversely, a client with **15 years with State Farm and no lapse** will get the best “persistency” or prior insurance discounts from many carriers, yielding lower quotes. Another example: marking “Currently have a homeowners policy with XYZ” can give a typical auto discount of perhaps 5-10%. If using PL Rating’s combined quote feature, that “account credit” is automatically applied (the Hanover PL Rating tips explicitly note that in package quoting, the system gives the account credit by default). If for some reason the client opts not to actually bundle at purchase, the agent would have to remove that credit (which would raise the premium).

At this stage, the agent has filled in all necessary fields. They would then click **Rate** or **Submit** in PL Rating, and the system will transmit this data to the selected insurance carriers’ rating engines and return quote premiums. The agent can then review the **Quote Results** screen, comparing prices and coverage variations. They might adjust some inputs (coverage limits, deductibles, etc.) and re-rate to fine-tune quotes or satisfy any carrier-specific missing info. For instance, a carrier might return a message “Roof age required for Home quote” or “Driver’s license number invalid format” – the agent would fix these, then re-run as needed.

## Homeowners Insurance – PL Rating Quoting Workflow

Quoting a **homeowners (HO) policy** in PL Rating follows a similar structured approach, with sections for applicant info, property details, coverages, and prior loss history. Home insurance requires more detailed property information (construction, features, etc.) than auto, so the data entry is extensive. The agent’s goal is to provide a complete picture of the home’s characteristics so that carriers can accurately calculate a dwelling replacement cost and premium. We will break down the process and highlight how answers (e.g. age of roof) can change outcomes, as well as differences in **California vs. Texas** home insurance scenarios.

### Applicant and Property Location Information

First, the agent enters the **client (applicant) information and property address** for the home policy. If this quote is part of a bundle with auto, some of the basic info may carry over. Key pieces here:

- **Named Insured(s) and Contact Info:** The homeowner’s name, and usually a second named insured if applicable (often a spouse or domestic partner), go on the application. In PL Rating’s Client Info, the agent can add a co-applicant similar to auto. The address of the property to be insured is entered – if the mailing address is different, that can be specified too. It’s important to get the ZIP code correct as it ties to fire risk, territory rates, etc.
    
- **Occupancy and Use of Home:** The agent specifies how the home is occupied: primary residence, secondary (vacation) home, seasonal use, or tenant-occupied (rental). A standard Homeowners (HO3) policy is for owner-occupied primary residences; secondary/seasonal homes can often be written on an HO3 as well, but some carriers treat them differently. The rater will have an occupancy field. If “rental property” or “tenant occupied” were chosen, the system might actually require switching to a dwelling fire policy quote instead of homeowners (since HO3 is owner-occupied). So generally for a homeowners quote, the agent will mark it as a primary residence (or secondary if the client indeed has a second home). They will also indicate if the insured is the owner of the home (nearly always yes for HO).
    
- **Additional Interests:** If the client has a **mortgage** on the home, the agent may note that a lender will be added. While the lender’s name/address might not be needed for just a quote, knowing there’s a mortgage reminds the agent that the policy will need to include that interest (and sometimes carriers give a slight discount for mortgages because lenders enforce home maintenance – this is minor if at all). Travelers asks “Do you have a mortgage on the home?” as part of insurance history questions, likely just to prompt the appropriate follow-up.
    
- **Household Residents:** The quoting form might ask who lives in the house. The agent will confirm if the insured lives there full-time, and if there are any other occupants (like relatives, tenants, etc.). Number of residents isn’t usually a rating factor, but **presence of any business or roomers** is important. **Home business use:** The agent must indicate if the client conducts any business from the home (like a day care, a home office with client visits, etc.). Many carriers will still insure a home with an office exposure (often requiring a home business endorsement), but some activities (running a daycare or storefront) can make a home ineligible for a homeowners policy. PL Rating will include a question about business use: e.g. “Any business conducted on premises?” If “yes,” it might require details or automatically limit which carriers quote.
    
- **Pets – especially Dogs:** The agent will ask if the client has any dogs (or other potentially liability-raising pets like exotic animals). **Dog ownership** is a common underwriting question because certain breeds with aggressive reputations can cause carriers to decline coverage or exclude dog liability. Travelers’ checklist explicitly mentions the quote process may ask **whether you own a dog or any other pets**. If the client answers yes, some carriers will then require the breed or size. PL Rating might not capture breed specifics in the initial quote, but the agent may jot it down for later because if it’s a **pit bull, Rottweiler, etc.,** a number of standard insurers in both CA and TX will not insure without an exclusion (or at all). At minimum, having a dog increases liability risk – Plymouth Rock notes that owning certain breeds can affect the cost due to increased liability exposure. The agent’s “yes” on dog ownership might trigger an alert or just be relayed to carriers, some of which might respond with higher quotes or refer conditions.
    
- **Marital Status and Household Info:** Marital status is usually already known from the auto policy if bundled, but if quoting standalone home, the agent will note if the insured is married (often the spouse should be a named insured if they have ownership). Also, if the insured is retired or over a certain age, some carriers have a retiree discount for home policies, so that could come up (e.g. a question “Is the head of household age 55+ and retired?”).
    

**Flow dependencies:** Basic occupancy answers can change which policy forms or carriers apply. For example, if the agent accidentally indicated “tenant-occupied,” the rater might switch to a dwelling fire quote (different workflow) – so they must ensure it’s correctly set to owner-occupied for a homeowners quote. If **business use** is answered yes (say the client works from home or runs a small side business at home), some carriers may drop off. A minor home office (computer work, no clients coming over) is usually fine, but something like “I bake cakes at home and customers pick up” might trigger eligibility issues. The agent might then know to exclude certain carriers or prepare to add a business endorsement later. **Dog ownership** won’t typically change the premium in the quote phase (unless a carrier outright declines), but it’s noted for underwriting. However, a large aggressive breed could cause some carriers’ quotes to not appear if they simply won’t insure that risk – the agent might not even realize why unless it’s flagged. They may have to address that in underwriting after quoting.

**Example dependency:** If the client says **“I operate a daycare in my home for 5 children,”** and the agent indicates business on premises = yes (and perhaps specifies daycare in a free-form note), most standard carriers will **refuse to quote a homeowners policy** (daycare is a high liability exposure). The agent would likely get zero results from preferred carriers in PL Rating in that case, or a note saying risk ineligible. That is an extreme case. On the other hand, if the client simply has a **home office (remote employee)**, marking “business use: yes” but describing it as office work might still allow quotes, though the agent might need to add a home business endorsement at binding.

Also, consider **California vs. Texas**: not much difference in this basic info section, except perhaps **marital status** trends (no rating difference) and **household composition**. One nuance: California insurers might ask if any occupants are smokers (fire risk), though it’s not as common nowadays to rate on that – it could be a carrier-specific question. Texas doesn’t differ on that front.

### Home Construction and Property Details

This is the most detailed part: describing the dwelling’s construction, age, size, and features. The agent essentially provides data for a **replacement cost estimator (RCE)** to calculate Coverage A (dwelling coverage) and for underwriting to assess risk factors. PL Rating’s interface will have a series of fields covering the following:

- **Year Built:** The year the home was originally built is fundamental. It sets the **age of the home**, which affects everything from base rates to required updates. For instance, a 1900-built home vs a 2015-built home will have different risk considerations. Insurers often have cutoffs for older homes needing updates (e.g. wiring, roofing). The year is entered and if it’s beyond a certain age (often 25-30+ years old), additional questions appear about updates (see below).
    
- **Construction Type:** Whether the house is **frame construction (wood)**, **masonry (brick)**, or other (stucco on frame, log, etc.). Many forms break this into **Construction Type** and **Exterior Walls**. For example, PL Rating might have “Frame” or “Joisted Masonry” as construction type, and a separate field for exterior siding material. The Canopy data shows _Frame Type: Wood_ and _Wall Type: Brick_ for a sample home. This indicates the house has brick veneer over a wood frame. If it were solid masonry, wall type might be “Masonry” and frame type “N/A.” These details affect fire resistance and replacement cost. In California, many homes are wood frame with stucco or siding; in Texas, you also have brick veneer homes – each has slight rating differences (brick is often a tad cheaper to insure because of better fire resistance).
    
- **Home Style and Stories:** The form may ask the **style of home** (colonial, ranch, split-level, etc.) or at least whether it’s 1-story, 2-story, etc. Style and number of stories affect rebuild cost (two-story homes cost a bit more per square foot sometimes). The Travelers checklist notes questions on **the style of the home and exterior finish**. The Plymouth Rock list asks for **number of stories** and total **square footage**. The agent will input the square footage of the living area, as that’s crucial for replacement cost. They’ll also input the number of **families/units** if it’s a multi-family (most HO policies are single-family, but duplex (2-unit) or tri-plex could be an HO3 if owner occupies one unit – some carriers allow that). The data snippet showed _Units: 1_ for a single-family home.
    
- **Square Footage & Rooms:** Total square footage of the house (above grade) is entered. Additionally, details like number of **bedrooms, bathrooms (full/half)**, maybe number of **fireplaces**, and any special rooms (finished basement, etc.) are collected. These are used to refine the replacement cost estimate. For example, Travelers mentions the quote will likely ask for the number of bathrooms and fireplaces. If the home has a finished basement or other notable features (like high-end kitchen, etc.), some systems include questions on those in an “upgrades” section.
    
- **Roof Details:** The type of roof is critical. The agent inputs the **roof material** (asphalt shingles, tile, metal, wood shake, etc.) and possibly the **roof shape** (gable, hip, flat). The Canopy data shows _Roof Cover: Single_ (likely an abbreviation or typo for “Shingle”) and _Roof Shape: Pyramid_ (a type of hip roof). Roof shape matters because a hip roof is more wind-resistant than a gable roof – insurers often give a discount in hurricane-prone areas for hip roofs. The **age of the roof** is extremely important too. The agent will either input the year the roof was last replaced or the roof’s age in years. Carriers care if a roof is new, since a **newer roof can significantly lower premiums** (due to less risk of leaks and better wind resistance). For example, replacing a roof can reduce premiums by **10–20% or more** in some cases. Many companies give a “new roof” discount if the roof is under a certain age (often 5 or 10 years). So if the client says “we put on a new roof 2 years ago,” the agent will input that – yielding a discount. Conversely, an **older roof** can trigger issues: insurers may mandate actual cash value coverage or decline risks when roofs get past 15-20 years without replacement. We’ll discuss that in the context of Texas wind/hail in a moment.
    
- **Update Questions (Wiring, Plumbing, etc.):** If the home is older (typically >25-30 years old), the application will ask if major systems have been updated:
    
    - **Electrical system:** Does the home have circuit breakers or old fuse box? Any aluminum wiring or knob-and-tube? If the wiring was updated, what year?
        
    - **Plumbing system:** Are there any old pipes (galvanized steel, for example) or has it been updated to copper/PVC and in what year? How old is the water heater? Travelers specifically notes questions like _“How old is your water heater? When did you last do major plumbing work?”_.
        
    - **Heating & AC:** What type of heating (gas furnace, electric, etc.) and was the furnace/HVAC updated and when? Is there central air conditioning? (Presence of **central HVAC** can impact cost – homes with central heat and air are viewed as standard, whereas a home with say wood stoves might be a fire hazard).
        
    - **Roof update:** (As already covered – year roof replaced is usually one of the update questions if original roof is old).
        
    
    These update questions allow carriers to gauge if an older home has been modernized. For example, a 1940 home fully updated in 2015 is a much better risk than one still with 1940s wiring and plumbing. If updates are not done and the home is very old, some insurers in both CA and TX might only offer a limited policy or decline. The agent collects as much info on updates as possible. Allstate notes these types of questions about renovations: _“Have you replaced the roof or done major renovations? How recently?”_ and _“How old is plumbing? Custom remodels?”_. This is to ensure the coverage reflects the current home’s value and condition.
    
- **Construction Features:** Additional details often include:
    
    - **Foundation type:** slab, crawlspace, basement. (Basements add cost in RCEs, and in TX many homes are slab-on-grade versus in CA you see more slab or crawl but few basements due to quake risk).
        
    - **Garage:** Attached vs detached, and how many car capacity. E.g. “Garage: attached 2-car, 400 sq ft.” The data snippet had _Garage Type: Attached, 1-Car_ and _Garage Sq Ft: 240_.
        
    - **Porches, decks:** Some forms will ask total square footage of porches or decks, or at least if there is a porch/deck, as it adds to replacement cost.
        
    - **Interior details:** High-level indicators like floor material (hardwood, carpet?), any custom features (vaulted ceilings, etc.) may be asked if using a detailed RCE, but in a quick quote the agent might skip that level. However, **number of kitchens** (1 or 2 if multi-family) and whether there’s a **fireplace** (yes, how many) are commonly asked. The Travelers checklist explicitly lists fireplaces as a detail to gather.
        
    - **Safety systems:** Smoke detectors, fire extinguishers, home security system, fire sprinkler system. These are usually asked because they can earn discounts. Travelers asks if you have safety devices or _smart home technology, security system, fire sprinklers_ – which may qualify for discounts. The agent will check off things like “central station burglar alarm” or “fire alarm monitored” if the client has them, which many carriers give a credit for (often ~5% discount for centrally monitored alarms).
        
    - **Swimming Pool or Trampoline:** Many home apps ask if there is a swimming pool on premises, and if yes, is it fenced and does it have a diving board or slide. Pools increase liability risk, so carriers want to know. Likewise, a trampoline on the property is a liability concern. The Plymouth Rock mention of “lifestyle choices” specifically calls out **having a swimming pool** or certain dog breeds can increase liability risk (and thus premiums). So an agent will usually note if a pool is present. If yes, they’ll later ensure the client has adequate liability coverage and maybe an umbrella. Some carriers might not quote homes with an unfenced pool. PL Rating likely includes “Pool: yes/no” and if yes maybe a follow-up about fencing.
        
- **Fire Protection Class:** A crucial factor for home insurance is how close the home is to fire-fighting resources. The agent will usually input:
    
    - **Distance to nearest fire hydrant** (in feet, or <1000 feet, etc.).
        
    - **Distance to fire station** (in miles). Many carriers require that the home be within e.g. 5 miles of a responding fire station and within 1000 feet of a hydrant to qualify for preferred rates. If it’s further, the Protection Class goes up (worse) and the premium surges or some carriers won’t write it. Plymouth Rock lists _“Distance to fire station”_ as a key rating factor and explains that a home 1 mile from a station will likely cost less to insure than one 15 miles away. The agent must input these distances. If the client isn’t sure, the agent might use mapping tools. In PL Rating, entering these distances helps determine the **Fire Protection Class (ISO PPC)** which underpins the base rate. California brush areas often have higher protection classes due to limited fire resources.
        
    - Additionally, whether the responding fire department is **paid or volunteer** can be a factor (volunteer departments might mean slower response).
        
    - **Hydrant**: If no hydrant within say 1000 feet, that’s a problem for many standard carriers. The agent would note if none is close (some forms ask “>1000 ft?” yes/no).
        

**Flow dependencies:** The property details directly determine the **Coverage A (Dwelling) replacement cost** that PL Rating calculates or the agent inputs. Many comparative raters have a built-in replacement cost calculator that uses all the above info (square footage, construction type, etc.) to estimate rebuilding cost. For example, after inputting data, PL Rating might suggest that the home’s replacement cost is $300,000, and it will pre-fill Coverage A = $300,000 (though the agent can tweak it if they have their own estimate or if the client wants a specific amount). If the agent enters a **very low Coverage A** that seems inconsistent with the data (say the home details imply $500k but agent put $300k), some carriers may not quote or will insure only up to a certain percentage – insurers want proper insurance-to-value. Conversely, if the agent demands a very high Coverage A (beyond what the data suggests), the quotes may be higher and could trigger a re-evaluation by underwriters.

Certain answers can **exclude carriers or require special policies**. For instance:

- If **roof material** is wood shake (high fire hazard) in a California wildfire zone, many standard carriers will not quote. The agent might then only see quotes from surplus line or the FAIR Plan scenario (which PL Rating typically doesn’t handle – FAIR Plan is a last resort market for fire coverage). Recently, carriers also ask about _wildfire mitigation_ (like clearing brush, fire-resistant materials) in CA due to new regulations, but that might be outside the PL Rating standard fields and more in carrier-specific questions.
    
- **Roof age dependency:** This is a big one. If the roof is beyond a certain age, some carriers will either **apply an ACV roof endorsement** or **not quote at all** until it’s replaced. In Texas especially, after severe hail claims in recent years, many insurers now default to **Actual Cash Value (ACV) coverage for roofs older than 10-15 years**. For example, a Texas insurer might quote a 20-year-old roof only with ACV (meaning depreciated value in a claim) or require a higher wind/hail deductible. The agent should be aware: _once a roof passes about 15 years in TX, expect premium increases or coverage limitations_. Indeed, as one Texas agency notes, _“Once your roof is over 10–15 years old, many Texas insurers will increase your premium, require ACV instead of replacement cost, or even deny roof coverage unless it’s replaced. Roofs over 20 years old often require proof of good condition or face non-renewal.”_. In PL Rating, the agent’s input of roof age will influence carriers: some may still quote replacement cost but at a higher rate, others might only offer a policy with an ACV roof clause (which might come through as a note or endorsement on the quote). If the roof was **just replaced (age 0-5 years)**, the agent will definitely indicate that, yielding new roof credits that reduce the premium (often a sizable discount).
    
- **Home age and updates:** If the home was built in, say, 1920 and the agent indicates **no updates** to wiring or plumbing, a lot of carriers will flat-out decline (due to potential for old knob-and-tube wiring or old pipes). The rater might then show quotes only from specialty or high-risk markets or might require the agent to change something (like if possible, “Fuse box: updated to breakers in 1990” – if the client knows any partial updates, it helps). Conversely, indicating **system updates** (e.g. “Electrical updated in 2018, Plumbing in 2010, Roof in 2019”) will **broaden the market of carriers** willing to quote and often lower premiums because the house is less likely to have fire or water issues.
    
- **Protection features:** If the agent checks “central fire alarm and sprinkler system,” some carriers will apply credits. If they check “no smoke detectors,” that could even be an issue (most carriers require at least basic smoke alarms operational). Usually, clients have those, so “yes” is expected.
    
- **Fire station distance:** If the agent inputs “10 miles to fire station” and “no hydrant nearby,” many standard insurers (especially in brush or rural areas of CA) won’t quote, as that’s a Protection Class 9 or 10 scenario. In Texas, rural ranch properties similarly could be tough. PL Rating will still try to return quotes, but they might be extremely high or from surplus markets. On the flip side, a home within **1 mile of a fire station and 500 feet of a hydrant** will get the best Protection Class (e.g. Class 1 in some areas) leading to lower rates.
    

**Example dependencies:** A clear example is **roof age in Texas**. Suppose an agent inputs that the roof was last replaced 20 years ago on a Texas home. Many carriers will interpret that as a substantial risk for hail/wind damage. As a result, the quotes might come back with an **ACV roof endorsement** (meaning any roof claim will be depreciated) or a **higher wind/hail deductible** (like 2% of dwelling value) by default. Some carriers may not quote at all until that roof is replaced. If the agent instead could say the roof was replaced, even partially, in the last 5-10 years, it would keep more carriers in play and yield better rates.

Another example: **Square footage and features affecting replacement cost.** If the agent undershoots the square footage or doesn’t mention a finished basement, the calculated Coverage A might be too low. This could slip through initially, but at binding or inspection the carrier would adjust it (possibly increasing premium). It’s best practice for the agent to be thorough: e.g., if the client finished their basement or added an addition, include that. Travelers’ guide asks specifically about upgrades like a **finished basement or kitchen remodel** because it raises the home’s replacement value. By capturing that (e.g., “Basement: finished, 800 sq ft”), the rater increases the replacement cost and thus the Coverage A. It might make the quote higher, but it ensures proper coverage. If the agent left it out, the quote might look cheap but the coverage would be insufficient.

### Coverage Selection (Homeowners)

After detailing the property, the agent will set the desired **coverage limits and endorsements** for the homeowners policy. This includes the dwelling coverage amount and the associated coverages B, C, D, as well as liability, medical payments, and deductibles. In PL Rating, this is usually the **Coverage Options** or **Policy Coverages** section. Key coverages:

- **Dwelling Coverage (Coverage A):** This is the amount of insurance on the house itself. Often, as mentioned, this will be calculated based on the replacement cost estimator. The agent can also input a specific Coverage A if the client has a figure in mind (like from another RCE or insurance valuation). Ideally, Coverage A should equal the cost to rebuild the home at today’s costs. The quote will heavily depend on this – it’s essentially the insured value. If the agent feels the RCE number is too low/high, they might adjust it and perhaps note an endorsement like **extended replacement cost**. In CA, many policies include **Extended Replacement Cost** (like +25% or +50% additional coverage if costs surge). The agent can select an endorsement for 125% or 150% of Coverage A if the client wants that extra buffer (some carriers include it in certain packages by default). In Texas, extended replacement isn’t as common (some offer it), but with inflation these days it’s more in use everywhere.
    
- **Other Structures (Coverage B):** Typically 10% of Coverage A by default for detached structures (shed, fence, etc.). The rater will either automatically set this (most carriers fix it at 10% on a standard HO3, though some allow adjusting higher). If the client has significant other structures (say a big detached garage or barn), the agent might increase Coverage B. There will be a field for that percentage or amount.
    
- **Personal Property (Coverage C):** Personal property (contents) is usually a percentage of Coverage A (50%–75% standard). Many homeowners policies default to 50% of dwelling for contents for owner-occupied homes, though some carriers default to 70%. PL Rating will likely default Coverage C = 50% of A or allow the agent to set the percentage. If the client has a lot of personal property or prefers a higher amount, the agent can raise it. In CA, some high-value carriers might use higher percentages. The agent will also consider if the client wants **Replacement Cost on Contents** (most do; it might be an option to add if not automatic).
    
- **Loss of Use (Coverage D):** This covers additional living expenses if the home is uninhabitable after a claim. Typically 20% of Coverage A is common. The rater will either default it or let the agent set it. It’s often not something the client changes – it’s proportionate.
    
- **Personal Liability:** The agent selects a liability limit (this covers bodily injury or property damage to others, e.g. someone suing after getting hurt on your property). Common options are $100,000, $300,000, $500,000. Many agents default to **$300,000 liability** nowadays as it’s only a few dollars more than $100k in most cases. If the client has a dog, pool, or high net worth, the agent will definitely recommend $300k or $500k. Umbrella policies start at $300k underlying usually. So PL Rating might default to $300k; the agent can adjust as needed. _State nuance:_ There’s no difference between CA and TX law here, but anecdotally, due to litigation climates one might consider higher limits in CA. In Texas, with higher incidence of dog bite claims and swimming pools (lots of pools in the Sunbelt), high liability is likewise recommended. The Plymouth Rock “lifestyle” mention underscores that **having a pool or certain dog can increase your liability risk** – i.e. you’d want higher liability coverage in such cases. Carriers might not force that, but an agent will advise it.
    
- **Medical Payments:** Typically a small coverage for guest minor injuries. Options might be $1,000, $5,000, or $10,000. The agent will pick one (often $5k is common). This doesn’t hugely impact premium so it’s usually set at a reasonably high level for goodwill.
    
- **Deductibles:** The agent sets the base policy deductible (which applies to perils like fire, water damage, theft, etc.). Common choices: $1,000; some clients opt for $2,500 or higher to save money. A higher deductible lowers premium. In PL Rating, the default might be $1,000. If the client wants to see a lower premium, the agent might also try $2,500 or $5,000 and note the difference. In **Texas**, importantly, there is often a separate **Wind/Hail deductible** (especially in coastal or hail-prone areas). In many cases it’s expressed as a percentage of Coverage A (1% or 2% are common). The agent will specify that if applicable. For example, a Texas policy might have a 1% wind/hail deductible and a $1,000 “all other perils” deductible. In PL Rating’s coverage options, there could be fields to set a separate wind deductible. In **California**, separate wind deductibles are not typical (except earthquake, which is separate coverage). But California does sometimes have separate wildfire deductibles emerging, though still rare; at quoting time usually just one deductible for all perils (other than optional earthquake).
    
- **Endorsements and Additional Coverages:** The agent can add optional coverages:
    
    - **Water Backup** (sewer/drain backup coverage): Very commonly added endorsement, usually offers $5,000 or $10,000 coverage for basement/sewer backups. If the client wants it, the agent selects it in the rater’s optional coverages list.
        
    - **Extended Replacement Cost**: as mentioned, e.g. +25% or +50% on dwelling. Carriers like Safeco, etc., often have this.
        
    - **Scheduled Personal Property**: If the client has high-value jewelry, art, etc., the agent might need to get those items appraised and schedule them. Comparative raters usually don’t handle scheduling individual items in a quote request due to complexity – that’s often done after selecting a carrier. So the agent would likely not input that here beyond maybe indicating “Yes, will schedule $20k jewelry” in a notes field.
        
    - **Earthquake Coverage:** In California, standard homeowners policies do _not_ include earthquake. Earthquake insurance is offered either via the **California Earthquake Authority (CEA)** or private quake insurers. PL Rating does **not** automatically quote earthquake as part of the HO policy (since it’s separate). The agent would discuss it separately. They might note if the client wants an earthquake quote – but that often requires a different system or going to CEA quoting. In Texas, earthquake is not a big concern (some might offer as an add-on if desired, but rare).
        
    - **Hurricane/Wind exclusions:** In Texas coastal counties, many carriers exclude windstorm coverage from the HO policy (homeowners in those areas must get a separate wind policy through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association or similar). In PL Rating, if the property is in a coastal ZIP (Tier 1 counties along the Gulf), the agent might have to check an option like “Exclude Wind Coverage?” for carriers that do so, or simply those carriers won’t return quotes unless wind is excluded. Some comparative raters have an option to quote _HO3 Wind Excluded_ vs _HO3 including Wind_. The agent would be mindful: if in Galveston or coastal Houston areas, likely they’ll be dealing with wind exclusions. For our purposes, just note that location can cause either included or excluded wind. Carriers that do write wind in those areas might have hefty hurricane deductibles. This is a Texas-specific flow dependency – in CA, wind is always included (the peril to worry about is wildfire, which is covered by fire coverage in HO; if carriers won’t write due to wildfire risk, that’s a declination rather than an excluded peril scenario).
        
    - **Building Ordinance or Law**: Many policies include 10% of Coverage A by default. Sometimes an option to increase to higher percentages (for older homes in CA, might want more ordinance coverage).
        
    - **Equipment Breakdown**: a newer optional add-on (covers HVAC/electrical system breakdown etc. for a small premium). Agent can opt to include if client interested.
        
    - **Identity Theft coverage** or **Service Line coverage**: other small endorsements that could be offered. PL Rating might not explicitly list every minor endorsement, but some do have checkboxes for popular ones like water backup, ID theft.
        
- **Policy Form / Package:** If quoting with certain carriers, they might have package tiers (like a standard vs enhanced homeowners package). For example, Hanover had Select, Select Plus, Prestige packages where various coverages are bundled. In PL Rating, the agent might pick one of those if available in carrier-specific questions. Otherwise, they set the individual coverages manually.
    

**Flow dependencies:** The coverage selections will influence price (obviously higher limits = higher premium) and sometimes **carrier eligibility**. For instance, some high-value carriers might require minimum Coverage A amounts (they won’t quote a low-value home). But more commonly, if a client wants a very high liability limit ($500k) only some carriers might offer that on an HO3 (others cap at $300k and would require an umbrella for more). Usually $500k is widely available though. If an agent doesn’t select water backup, a carrier that automatically includes some might have a higher quote vs one that doesn’t; these differences will be in the details.

For **state differences**: A big one is **wind/hail coverage in Texas**. If the property is near the coast, an agent might have to quote with wind excluded (meaning the premium shown is for fire/theft etc., but not hurricane – the client would then also get a separate wind policy quote). This complicates the workflow because PL Rating’s comparison might show some carriers “with wind” and some “without” – the agent must carefully note that. For inland Texas, wind is included but often with a percentage deductible. In California, **wildfire risk** is the elephant in the room – but that’s handled by carriers either quoting or not quoting. The agent may not have a separate “exclude fire” option (except going to FAIR Plan which is outside normal comparative rating). So the main coverage difference is earthquake is separate (usually not quoted here) and maybe building code upgrade coverage needs are higher in CA due to earthquake retrofitting costs (but that’s an advanced detail).

**Example dependencies:** If the agent chooses a **higher deductible** – say $2,500 instead of $500 – the premium might drop significantly (maybe ~10-15%). The client might want to see that difference. So an agent could run two quote scenarios in PL Rating with different deductibles as a comparison. Another example: adding **water backup coverage** for, say, $50/year more – a client with a basement in Texas might absolutely want it (due to heavy rainstorms). If the agent forgets to add it, the quote will be a bit lower, but the client would be missing coverage. Agents often include it by default if they know it’s important and mention the slight increase.

A **Texas-specific scenario**: Many Texas insurers apply a mandatory **1% wind/hail deductible** on all policies in hail-prone regions (which is most of TX). The agent in PL Rating might see that the quote comes with, for example, a $3,000 wind/hail deductible on a $300k house (1%). If the client wants a flat $1,000 deductible for everything, some carriers might allow that in non-coastal areas, but it could raise the premium or not be available. The rater’s output would include these details, and the agent would explain them.

One more conditional: If the client’s home is in a known flood zone, the agent will remind them that **flood insurance** is separate (and not handled by PL Rating for homeowner quotes). They might not input anything about flood in the rater (except maybe noting if the property is in a flood plain for underwriting). In TX, coastal homes might need flood and wind separate. In CA, certain high flood areas are less common but exist. Again, outside the direct quoting, but part of the workflow discussion the agent has with the client.

### Prior Claims History (Homeowners)

Just as with auto, the agent will provide the **loss history for the home**. Homeowners insurance carriers typically ask for any claims in the past 5 years (some ask 3 years) that the prospective insured has incurred, whether at this property or a previous property. PL Rating’s interface will have a section to input prior claims, usually including date, type of loss, and amount paid. The agent will query the client: “Any home insurance claims in recent years? Fire, water damage, theft, liability claims, etc.” Key points:

- **Losses at the property vs by the insured:** In homeowners, some carriers care if the property itself had certain losses (even if under a prior owner), but generally they look at the claims history of the insured (and any at this address under the insured’s ownership). The agent will list each claim the client had. For example: a $10k water damage claim 2 years ago, or a theft claim 4 years ago. The Canopy Connect example shows a **Homeowner Policy claims history** with entries like _12/18/2021 – Wind/Hail – $5,217 paid_ and _09/23/2019 – Fire – $12,255 paid_. The agent would input those if applicable.
    
- **Cause of loss:** Carriers differentiate between **catastrophe losses** (like wildfire, hurricane, hail – things largely out of homeowner’s control) and **non-catastrophe losses** (like a pipe leak or theft). Too many of either is bad, but multiple non-cat losses are especially red flags (could indicate maintenance issues or fraudulent behavior). For instance, Hanover’s Platinum eligibility required no more than 1 non-catastrophic loss in 5 years. The agent will indicate cause (the rater usually has categories: fire, water, theft, liability, weather, etc.). Plymouth Rock notes that _if you have not filed any claims in recent years, you may get a discount; if you have a history of multiple claims, insurers view the property as more susceptible to damage_. Many carriers indeed offer a **claims-free discount** (often ~5-10%). So a client with zero prior claims will get better pricing than one with even one prior claim.
    
- **Number of claims:** The agent will list them out. If the number is high (e.g. 3 claims in 5 years), a lot of carriers will decline to quote. PL Rating may still produce some quotes (from perhaps more expensive carriers). The agent might prepare the client that their options are limited.
    
- **Loss details:** If one of the losses was large or involved certain hazards (like a prior **water loss** is a big deal, since a home that had a water leak could be prone to mold or second leak), carriers may exclude or surcharge for water. The agent can’t directly show that in the rater beyond noting the claim, but the carrier responses might include an advisory or simply a higher rate. A prior **liability claim** (like someone got injured on your property and you were sued) would also scare some insurers. But those are less common.
    

**Flow dependencies:** Inputting prior claims will remove any “claims-free” discount in the quotes. For example, if claims-free discount was 10%, that is lost and premium goes up accordingly. Additionally, the presence of certain claims may cause some carriers to not quote. For instance, a home with **two water damage claims in 5 years** – many standard carriers will say no thanks. Or they might quote but with a **water damage exclusion or a much higher water deductible**. The agent may see a note like “Carrier X will offer policy with a water exclusion endorsement due to prior losses.” It then becomes a decision point. A **catastrophic loss** like one wildfire claim might not penalize as much as say two theft claims. But any loss can remove the incident-free discount. Plymouth Rock emphasizes that **the more claims filed over the years, the more an insurer will view the property as a risk and the higher the quote may be**. So a client with multiple past claims will see higher premiums from all quoting carriers.

If the client has **no prior home insurance** (say a first-time homebuyer or previously just a renter), there’s no claims history to report for a home policy (though the insurer might consider if the client had frequent renters insurance claims, but usually they don’t check that deeply in quoting). However, if they owned the home but let it go uninsured for a while (rare, especially if a mortgage exists requiring insurance), that could be a concern but usually the key is claims.

**Example dependency:** If the agent enters **“1 claim in last 5 years – Lightning fire, $20k paid”**, most carriers will still quote, but the premium might be a bit higher and that client won’t get a claims-free discount. If they enter **“3 claims in 3 years”** (perhaps one water, one theft, one hail), many carriers will simply not quote or will quote with surcharges. The rater might return only one or two options, at very high premiums, essentially indicating the client is in a high-risk pool now. Another example: **Texas specific** – Texas gets a lot of wind/hail claims; if the client had, say, two hail claims in the past 5 years, some insurers might be cautious but since hail is usually considered cat loss, they might still insure but with a higher deductible on wind or recommend roof inspection. If the agent notes those, carriers that suffered heavy hail losses might price high. Meanwhile in **California**, if a client had a **wildfire claim**, currently many insurers are pulling out of covering that area again – the rater may have few results for that address regardless of claims because of wildfire zone issues, but a prior fire claim would certainly be a flag.

At this point, the agent has filled in **Applicant info, Property info, Coverages, and Prior claims**. They would then hit the **Rate/Quote** button. PL Rating will connect to the various insurers’ systems and return quotes.

The agent will review the **homeowners quote results** similar to auto. They’ll see premiums and possibly some notes (like certain coverages or conditions). If some carriers require additional info (e.g. maybe one carrier wants the **Fireline score** for wildfire risk – if not provided, they might return a “Refer to Underwriting” instead of a firm quote). The agent might then know the quote isn’t final for those. Typically, the agent picks the best options and prepares to present them to the client, possibly adjusting coverages or re-quoting different scenarios as needed (e.g. “What if we go with a 2% wind deductible, how much do we save?” – they can tweak and rerun).

## Renewals, Endorsements, and Rewrites – Additional Context

Although PL Rating is primarily used for **new business quoting**, it’s helpful to understand how the workflow differs for **policy renewals, mid-term changes (endorsements), or rewriting a policy**:

- **Renewals:** When a policy comes up for renewal (annually or semi-annually for auto), the current insurer will typically provide a renewal offer directly. The agent doesn’t need to re-enter data into PL Rating just to renew with the same company. However, if the client’s renewal premium is high or they want to shop around, the agent can **re-market** the account using PL Rating. In that case, the workflow is essentially the same as new business: the agent would have the updated info (maybe the home’s rebuild cost has changed, or the driver turned 25, etc.) and run new comparative quotes to see if switching carriers makes sense. Often, agents do this every couple of years for clients to ensure competitive rates. If the client is happy and staying put, the renewal is processed via the carrier systems, not through PL Rating. It’s worth noting that after a new policy is written, the renewal will be automatically sent by the carrier, and the client can then decide to accept it or shop. The agent’s role at renewal might be advisory unless shopping is needed.
    
- **Endorsements (Mid-term Changes):** If the client needs to change something on an active policy (add a vehicle, change a roof update, etc.), that is done as a policy endorsement through the carrier or the agency management system, not through PL Rating. **PL Rating doesn’t process endorsements** – it’s a quoting tool, not a policy service tool. For example, if a client **buys a new car**, the agent will gather the new car info (VIN, coverages needed) and contact the carrier to add it to the existing policy. They might use PL Rating in a limited way to **estimate** the difference in premium by quoting the new car scenario, but the actual change is done via carrier portal or AMS download. As Baily Insurance describes, if you call your agent to add a teen driver or a new vehicle, the agent **“will take your information”** (license, DOB for the teen; or vehicle details) and then update your policy accordingly. This is usually quick since all other info is already on file. If the premium change is large, an agent might on occasion requote the whole account with other carriers (e.g. adding a teen made your current insurer double the rate, so they shop for alternatives – effectively that’s a new business quote again via PL Rating). But endorsement processing itself is outside PL Rating.
    
- **Rewrites:** A rewrite is essentially starting a new policy to replace an old one (often due to a lapse or non-renewal). If a policy cancels for non-payment and the client wants coverage again, some carriers allow a “rewrite” within a short window, otherwise it’s treated as new business. In either case, the agent could use PL Rating to get new quotes. If rewriting with the same insurer after a lapse, it might just be done in carrier system. If the carrier non-renewed the policy (common in CA now for wildfire areas), the agent will have to use PL Rating to find a new carrier willing to write the risk. In practice, a rewrite to a new insurer is just a new business quote and policy, so the PL Rating workflow is the same as discussed – the agent ensures all info is up to date (perhaps the roof was replaced since the last policy, etc.) and then quotes anew.
    

One thing to mention is **data pre-fill for renewals or rewrites**: If the agency has an integration, they might export client data from their management system into PL Rating to avoid re-typing. For example, Vertafore’s systems allow bridging existing client info into PL Rating for a quote. The agent then just updates anything new (like a different car or an updated roof age) and runs the quotes. This makes re-shopping at renewal faster.

In summary, **new policy quotes** (for new customers or new lines of business) are where PL Rating is heavily used. For **renewals**, agents use it selectively if shopping the account. And for in-force changes (**endorsements**), PL Rating isn’t used except to quote adding new coverages as a what-if. Instead, endorsements are handled directly with carriers, often in a matter of minutes by inputting the change and getting a premium adjustment.

If it has been a couple years since the last market comparison, agents often suggest re-quoting – _“If it’s been two or more years since we last quoted your policy, it’s a good idea to call and have us do a quote”_, since there may be new discounts or better rates. That implies using PL Rating again to check the market at renewal time.

---

**Conclusion:** By following the PL Rating workflow – entering applicant info, asset details, additional exposures (drivers or property features), selecting coverages, and disclosing history – an insurance producer in an independent agency can obtain multiple competitive quotes for personal auto and homeowners policies in **California, Texas, or any state**. Each grouping of information (drivers, vehicles, home construction, prior claims, etc.) is crucial to the accuracy of the quote. Small changes in answers can have significant impacts: for example, a **new roof** can yield sizable discounts in homeowners, a **shorter commute** lowers an auto premium, and a **clean driving record** or **claims-free history** unlocks preferred rates. State regulations and risks also shape the process – California’s laws ensure good drivers get discounts, while Texas insurers pay keen attention to hail-proof roofs and credit scores.

Ultimately, PL Rating streamlines the data entry so that once the producer inputs these fields, they can quickly compare carrier offerings and find the best fit for the client’s needs. The workflow we detailed is the backbone of quoting new policies, and understanding each question’s purpose (and the consequences of each answer) allows an agent to navigate the rating process efficiently and accurately.
