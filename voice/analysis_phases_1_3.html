3.3 — Deep Dive: Top Addressable Patterns

Pattern A: Perceived Dead Air / Latency (200 calls with "Hello?")

This is the single most impactful addressable issue. 200 English calls (22% of the entire cohort) contain the caller saying "Hello?" — indicating they perceive silence or a non-responsive line. In many cases, the AI's response arrives just as the caller is saying "Hello?", creating a talk-over effect that compounds the problem.

assistant: Hi - Thanks for calling Dale Wagner Insurance, this is Kelly on a recorded line. How can I help? user: Hello? assistant: Yeah, I'm here. What can I… [caller hangs up]
assistant: Hi - Thanks for calling Dale Wagner Insurance, this is Kelly on a recorded line. How can I help? user: Hello. assistant: Hi there. What can I help you with— user: Hello? [caller hangs up]

Pattern B: AI Detection & Distrust (172 calls)

When callers realize they're talking to an AI, the current response varies but often includes phrases like "I get that a lot" or "I'm actually a virtual assistant." Many callers hang up at this point, particularly when they need account-specific help that clearly requires a human.

user: Oh, it's— assistant: Yeah, go ahead. What— user: machine. assistant: Yeah, I get that a lot. I'm actually here to help with insurance questions… user: He… [caller hangs up]

Pattern C: Truncated / Cut-Off Responses (170 calls)

In 170 calls, the final message from the assistant ends mid-sentence — the caller hung up while the AI was still speaking. This strongly suggests the AI's response pace doesn't match caller expectations; by the time it starts answering, the caller has already decided to leave.

3.4 — Recommendations: Reducing Addressable Drop-Off

1. Reduce Response Latency / Add Filler Acknowledgments

Problem: Callers perceive dead air between their statement and the AI's response, leading to "Hello?" loops.

Recommendation: Insert immediate, ultra-short filler responses (under 500ms) before the substantive answer. Use phrases like "Mm-hmm," "Sure," or "Got it" as instant acknowledgments while the full response generates. This mirrors natural human phone behavior where we signal we're listening before forming a full reply.

Expected impact: Could reduce the 200+ "Hello?" pattern calls significantly. Even a 30% reduction would recover ~60 calls per quarter.

2. Redesign the AI Disclosure Response

Problem: When callers ask "is this a machine?", current responses like "I get that a lot" or "I'm actually a virtual assistant" lead to immediate hang-ups.

Recommendation: Pivot the response to lead with capability and value before the disclosure. Example: "I can help you with quotes, policy questions, claims, and more — and if you need a licensed agent, I can connect you right away. I am an AI assistant, but I handle most requests start to finish. What can I help you with?" The key is to answer the implicit question ("can you actually help me?") before the explicit one ("are you a robot?").

Expected impact: Of the 172 AI-detection drop-offs, many callers have legitimate needs. A value-first framing could retain 20–40% of them.

3. Improve the Opening Greeting for Faster Engagement

Problem: The current greeting ("Hi - Thanks for calling [Agency], this is Kelly on a recorded line. How can I help?") is long and formal. By the time the caller processes the recorded-line disclosure, there's already a perceived pause.

Recommendation: Shorten the greeting and front-load the interactive prompt: "Hi, this is Kelly at [Agency]. How can I help you today?" Move the recorded-line notice to a brief mention after the first exchange, or embed it as a quick aside. Shorter greetings get to the conversation faster and reduce the window where callers decide to hang up.

Expected impact: Incremental improvement across all calls, reducing the 6–10 second "dead zone" after the greeting.

4. Proactive "Let me transfer you" for Specific-Person Requests

Problem: When callers ask for a named person (163 instances), the AI often explains the office is closed before offering alternatives, which frustrates callers who just want to be connected.

Recommendation: When a caller names a specific person, immediately acknowledge and offer the next-best action: "Let me check — [Name] isn't available right now. I can take a message so they call you back, or I can help you with most insurance questions directly. Which would you prefer?" Lead with the action, not the obstacle.

Expected impact: Won't eliminate this inherent category, but a faster, more empathetic handoff could convert some of these into message-left or question-answered outcomes.

5. Streamline After-Hours Messaging Flow

Problem: Many callers who reach the AI after hours want to leave a simple message (112 calls). The current flow often asks clarifying questions before getting to the message-taking step, causing some to abandon.

Recommendation: When the office is closed, offer message-taking as the first option: "The office is closed right now — would you like to leave a message for a callback, or can I help you with something right now?" Make message-taking a one-step process: collect name, number, and brief note, then confirm.

Expected impact: Converts some of the voicemail-seeking drop-offs into captured messages.

6. Faster Spanish Language Routing

Problem: 69 English-cohort calls involved a language barrier. Some callers who need Spanish are going through the English greeting before switching, adding friction.

Recommendation: For agencies with significant Spanish-speaking caller bases, consider opening with a bilingual prompt that includes the Spanish option earlier: "Thanks for calling [Agency]. Para español, diga 'español.' For English, how can I help?"

Expected impact: Reduces confusion and early hang-ups from Spanish speakers who don't understand the English greeting.

Bottom line: Of the 533 short calls in the English cohort, roughly 380 (71%) have addressable elements. The three highest-leverage improvements are reducing perceived latency (Pattern A), redesigning the AI disclosure (Pattern B), and shortening the opening greeting (Pattern C). Together, these address the root causes behind the majority of addressable drop-off.
